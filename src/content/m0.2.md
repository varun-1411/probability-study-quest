## The story

Your transient solver computes the distribution of the taxi stand at time $t$ by uniformization:
$$p(t)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}e^{-\Lambda t}\frac{(\Lambda t)^n}{n!}\,p(0)P^n ,$$
and the code stops the sum at some $N$. Three questions follow at once.

1. Does the infinite sum mean anything, and how large is the error from stopping at $N$?
2. Is one $N$ good for every time of the day, or must $N$ grow with $t$?
3. Your optimizer needs the derivative of an expected cost with respect to a release rate or a price $\theta$. May you differentiate inside the sum, or inside an integral over the day?

Real analysis is the subject that answers questions like these. Every big theorem in probability (the laws of large numbers, the central limit theorem, monotone and dominated convergence) says that some limit exists, or that two limits can be taken in either order. This module gives you the tools:

- **completeness**: limits exist even when you cannot write them down;
- **sequences and series**: infinite sums have a meaning, and sometimes the order of terms matters;
- **compactness**: optimal decisions exist;
- **uniform convergence**: limits, integrals and derivatives can be swapped.

## The picture

Draw these three before you read on.

1. Dots $x_1,x_2,\dots$ on a line, a horizontal band of half-width $\varepsilon$ around a value $x$, and a mark at the index $N$ after which every dot stays inside the band. Shrink $\varepsilon$: the mark moves right. That is convergence.
2. The graphs of $x^n$ on $[0,1]$ for $n=1,2,5,20$. They hug $0$, then shoot up to $1$ at $x=1$. Draw a band of half-width $\tfrac12$ around the limit function ($0$ on $[0,1)$, $1$ at $1$): no graph fits inside it. That is convergence at every point that is **not uniform**.
3. A tent of height $n$ on the base $[0,2/n]$. Its area is $1$ for every $n$, yet at every fixed point it eventually becomes $0$. The limit of the areas ($1$) is not the area of the limit ($0$).

## 1. The real numbers and completeness

The rationals have gaps: no rational number squares to $2$ (Module 0.1). The real numbers fill them, and this one axiom says how.

**Axiom of completeness.** Every nonempty set $A\subseteq\mathbb R$ that is bounded above has a least upper bound, written $\sup A$.

Likewise every nonempty set bounded below has a greatest lower bound $\inf A=-\sup(-A)$.

**Lemma 1.1 (how to use a sup).** Let $s$ be an upper bound of $A$. Then $s=\sup A$ if and only if for every $\varepsilon>0$ there is $a\in A$ with $a>s-\varepsilon$.

*Proof.* If $s=\sup A$ and $\varepsilon>0$, then $s-\varepsilon$ is smaller than the least upper bound, so it is not an upper bound: some $a\in A$ exceeds it. Conversely, suppose the condition holds and $b<s$ is another upper bound. Take $\varepsilon=s-b$: some $a\in A$ has $a>s-\varepsilon=b$, a contradiction. So no upper bound is smaller than $s$. $\blacksquare$

A sup need not be attained: $\sup\{1-1/n:n\in\mathbb N\}=1$, but no element equals $1$. When the sup is attained it is a **maximum**. At the stand, "the longest wait among today's passengers" is a maximum over a finite list. "The lowest average wait over all release rates $\theta>0$" is an infimum, and no rate may achieve it. Section 6 says when one does.

**Archimedean property.** $\mathbb N$ is not bounded above; equivalently, for every $\varepsilon>0$ there is $n$ with $1/n<\varepsilon$ (Problem B1). It implies that $\mathbb Q$ is **dense**: between any two real numbers lies a rational.

## 2. Sequences

**Definition.** $x_n\to x$ if for every $\varepsilon>0$ there is $N$ such that $|x_n-x|<\varepsilon$ for all $n\ge N$.

The basic facts, all proved in Abbott Section 2.3: limits are unique; a convergent sequence is bounded; limits respect sums, products, quotients (when the limit of the denominator is not $0$) and inequalities, so $x_n\le y_n$ for all $n$ gives $\lim x_n\le\lim y_n$; and the **squeeze** rule holds. As a sample, boundedness: with $\varepsilon=1$ there is $N$ with $|x_n|<|x|+1$ for $n\ge N$, so $|x_n|\le\max\{|x_1|,\dots,|x_{N-1}|,|x|+1\}$ for every $n$.

**Theorem 2.1 (monotone convergence).** An increasing sequence that is bounded above converges, and its limit is $\sup_n x_n$.

*Proof.* Let $s=\sup_n x_n$, which exists by completeness. Given $\varepsilon>0$, Lemma 1.1 gives $N$ with $x_N>s-\varepsilon$. For $n\ge N$, $s-\varepsilon<x_N\le x_n\le s$. $\blacksquare$

This gives a limit without telling you what it is. In probability it becomes continuity of probability along increasing events, and later the monotone convergence theorem for integrals.

**Theorem 2.2 (Bolzano–Weierstrass).** Every bounded sequence has a convergent subsequence.

*Proof.* Call an index $m$ a *peak* if $x_m\ge x_n$ for every $n>m$. If there are infinitely many peaks $m_1<m_2<\cdots$, then $x_{m_1}\ge x_{m_2}\ge\cdots$ is a decreasing subsequence. If there are finitely many, pick $n_1$ beyond the last one. Since $n_1$ is not a peak, some $n_2>n_1$ has $x_{n_2}>x_{n_1}$; $n_2$ is not a peak either, and so on. This builds an increasing subsequence. Either way the subsequence is monotone and bounded, so it converges by Theorem 2.1 (for the decreasing case apply the theorem to $-x_n$). $\blacksquare$

**Cauchy sequences.** $(x_n)$ is *Cauchy* if for every $\varepsilon>0$ there is $N$ with $|x_m-x_n|<\varepsilon$ for all $m,n\ge N$. The terms get close to *each other*; no limit is mentioned.

**Theorem 2.3.** A sequence of real numbers converges if and only if it is Cauchy (Problem B3).

This is the test you use when the limit is unknown: value iteration, a fixed-point solver (Problem C3), the partial sums of a series. It fails in $\mathbb Q$: the decimal truncations $1,\ 1.4,\ 1.41,\ 1.414,\dots$ of $\sqrt2$ are Cauchy, but they have no rational limit. "Cauchy sequences converge" is exactly what completeness means, and Section 6 uses it as the definition in other spaces.

## 3. limsup and liminf

Many sequences in probability never settle down but still have useful long-run bounds; the fraction of blocked hours up to day $n$ can oscillate forever. Define
$$y_n=\sup_{k\ge n}x_k,\qquad z_n=\inf_{k\ge n}x_k .$$
As $n$ grows you take the sup over fewer terms, so $y_n$ decreases, and $z_n$ increases. Their limits always exist in $[-\infty,\infty]$ by Theorem 2.1:
$$\limsup_{n}x_n=\lim_n y_n=\inf_n\sup_{k\ge n}x_k,\qquad \liminf_n x_n=\lim_n z_n=\sup_n\inf_{k\ge n}x_k .$$

The facts you will use:

- $\liminf x_n\le\limsup x_n$, because $z_n\le y_n$.
- $x_n\to L$ if and only if $\liminf x_n=\limsup x_n=L$ (Problem B4).
- If $L=\limsup x_n$ is finite, then for every $\varepsilon>0$: $x_n<L+\varepsilon$ for all large $n$, and $x_n>L-\varepsilon$ for infinitely many $n$. So $\limsup$ is the largest limit of any subsequence.

**Back to Module 0.1.** For events $A_n$, the indicators $\mathbf 1_{A_n}(\omega)$ form a $0$–$1$ sequence for each outcome $\omega$. Its limsup is $1$ exactly when it equals $1$ infinitely often, that is, when $\omega\in\limsup_n A_n$. So
$$\mathbf 1_{\limsup_n A_n}=\limsup_n\mathbf 1_{A_n},\qquad \mathbf 1_{\liminf_n A_n}=\liminf_n\mathbf 1_{A_n}.$$
The set operation from Module 0.1 is the number operation applied to indicators.

## 4. Series

$\sum a_n$ **converges** if the partial sums $s_n=a_1+\dots+a_n$ converge. Theorem 2.3 turns into the **Cauchy criterion**: $\sum a_n$ converges if and only if for every $\varepsilon>0$ there is $N$ with $|a_{m+1}+\dots+a_n|<\varepsilon$ whenever $n>m\ge N$.

Consequences:

- If $\sum a_n$ converges then $a_n\to0$ (take $n=m+1$). The converse is false: in the harmonic series $1+\tfrac12+(\tfrac13+\tfrac14)+(\tfrac15+\dots+\tfrac18)+\cdots$ every bracket is at least $\tfrac12$, so the sum diverges.
- **Comparison.** If $0\le a_n\le b_n$ and $\sum b_n$ converges, so does $\sum a_n$: its partial sums increase and are bounded (Theorem 2.1).
- **Geometric series.** $\sum_{n\ge0}r^n=1/(1-r)$ for $|r|<1$.
- **Absolute convergence.** If $\sum|a_n|<\infty$ then $\sum a_n$ converges, because $|a_{m+1}+\dots+a_n|\le|a_{m+1}|+\dots+|a_n|$.
- **Ratio test.** If $|a_{n+1}/a_n|\to\rho<1$ the series converges absolutely; if $\rho>1$ it diverges.

**Order matters unless convergence is absolute.** The series $1-\tfrac12+\tfrac13-\tfrac14+\cdots$ converges (to $\ln 2$) but not absolutely. Riemann's rearrangement theorem says its terms can be reordered to sum to any number you like. An absolutely convergent series gives the same sum in every order (Problem B5). This is why $E[X]=\sum_k x_k\,p_k$ for a discrete random variable is defined only when $\sum_k|x_k|\,p_k<\infty$: the values of $X$ come in no natural order, so the answer must not depend on one (Problem I2).

**The Poisson tail, which you will use all year.** $\sum_{n\ge0}\lambda^n/n!=e^\lambda$, so the numbers $e^{-\lambda}\lambda^n/n!$ are probabilities: the Poisson($\lambda$) distribution.

**Lemma 4.1 (Poisson tail bound).** Let $\lambda>0$ and let $N$ be an integer with $N+2>\lambda$. Put $r=\lambda/(N+2)$. Then
$$\sum_{n=N+1}^{\infty}e^{-\lambda}\frac{\lambda^n}{n!}\;\le\;e^{-\lambda}\frac{\lambda^{N+1}}{(N+1)!}\cdot\frac{1}{1-r}.$$

*Proof.* Write $t_n=e^{-\lambda}\lambda^n/n!$. For $n\ge N+1$, $t_{n+1}/t_n=\lambda/(n+1)\le\lambda/(N+2)=r<1$. By induction, $t_n\le t_{N+1}r^{\,n-N-1}$ for $n\ge N+1$. Summing the geometric series, $\sum_{n\ge N+1}t_n\le t_{N+1}\sum_{j\ge0}r^j=t_{N+1}/(1-r)$. $\blacksquare$

This is the bound behind the truncation level $N$ in your uniformization code (Problems T1 and D1). It is sharp: once $N$ is somewhat above $\lambda$, it is within a few percent of the exact tail (Problem A6).

## 5. Continuous functions

$f:A\to\mathbb R$ is **continuous at** $c\in A$ if for every $\varepsilon>0$ there is $\delta>0$ such that $x\in A$ and $|x-c|<\delta$ give $|f(x)-f(c)|<\varepsilon$.

**Sequential form.** $f$ is continuous at $c$ if and only if $f(x_n)\to f(c)$ for every sequence $x_n\to c$ in $A$ (Abbott Section 4.3). This is the version you will use most.

**Theorem 5.1 (extreme value theorem).** A continuous function on $[a,b]$ is bounded and attains its maximum and its minimum.

*Proof.* *Bounded:* if not, pick $x_n\in[a,b]$ with $|f(x_n)|>n$. By Bolzano–Weierstrass a subsequence $x_{n_k}\to c$, and $c\in[a,b]$ because $a\le x_{n_k}\le b$. Continuity gives $f(x_{n_k})\to f(c)$; a convergent sequence is bounded, but $|f(x_{n_k})|>n_k\to\infty$. *Maximum:* let $M=\sup f([a,b])$, which is finite. By Lemma 1.1 pick $x_n$ with $f(x_n)>M-1/n$. A subsequence converges to some $c\in[a,b]$, and from $M-1/n_k<f(x_{n_k})\le M$ and $f(x_{n_k})\to f(c)$ we get $f(c)=M$. *Minimum:* apply this to $-f$. $\blacksquare$

**Intermediate value theorem.** A continuous $f$ on $[a,b]$ takes every value between $f(a)$ and $f(b)$ (Abbott Section 4.5). You will use it to show that a quantile exists, or that some price makes expected demand hit a target.

**Uniform continuity.** $f$ is *uniformly continuous* on $A$ if one $\delta$ works at every point: for every $\varepsilon>0$ there is $\delta>0$ such that $x,y\in A$ and $|x-y|<\delta$ give $|f(x)-f(y)|<\varepsilon$. The function $x^2$ is continuous on $\mathbb R$ but not uniformly continuous: $x^2-y^2=(x-y)(x+y)$ is large for large $x$ even when $x-y$ is small.

**Theorem 5.2.** A continuous function on $[a,b]$ is uniformly continuous.

*Proof.* If not, there are $\varepsilon>0$ and points $x_n,y_n\in[a,b]$ with $|x_n-y_n|<1/n$ but $|f(x_n)-f(y_n)|\ge\varepsilon$. A subsequence $x_{n_k}\to c\in[a,b]$, and then $y_{n_k}\to c$ too. By continuity at $c$, $f(x_{n_k})-f(y_{n_k})\to f(c)-f(c)=0$, a contradiction. $\blacksquare$

**Mean value theorem.** If $f$ is continuous on $[a,b]$ and differentiable on $(a,b)$, then $f(b)-f(a)=f'(\xi)(b-a)$ for some $\xi\in(a,b)$ (Abbott Section 5.3). Consequence: if $|f'|\le L$ on an interval, then $|f(x)-f(y)|\le L|x-y|$ there. This is how you bound errors and prove that an iteration contracts (Problem C3).

## 6. Metric spaces and compactness

The same ideas work in other spaces: probability vectors, functions of time, policies.

A **metric** on a set $X$ is a function $d$ with $d(x,y)\ge0$, equality only when $x=y$, $d(x,y)=d(y,x)$, and the triangle inequality $d(x,z)\le d(x,y)+d(y,z)$. Examples:

- $\mathbb R^m$ with the Euclidean distance, or with $\|x-y\|_1=\sum_i|x_i-y_i|$. For two probability vectors, half the $\ell_1$ distance is the **total variation distance**: the largest difference the two distributions assign to any event.
- Bounded functions on a set $A$ with $d_\infty(f,g)=\sup_{x\in A}|f(x)-g(x)|$. Convergence in $d_\infty$ is uniform convergence (Section 8).

Convergence, Cauchy sequences and closed sets carry over word for word with $d(x,y)$ in place of $|x-y|$. A set $F$ is **closed** if it contains the limit of every convergent sequence of points of $F$. $X$ is **complete** if every Cauchy sequence converges to a point of $X$. $\mathbb R$ and $\mathbb R^m$ are complete and $\mathbb Q$ is not. The continuous functions on $[a,b]$ with $d_\infty$ are complete: this follows from the Cauchy criterion for uniform convergence and Theorem 8.1 (Abbott Section 6.2).

**Compactness.** $K\subseteq X$ is **compact** if every sequence in $K$ has a subsequence that converges to a point of $K$. (In metric spaces this is equivalent to the open-cover definition in Rudin chapter 2.)

**Heine–Borel.** In $\mathbb R^m$, a set is compact if and only if it is closed and bounded. For "if": apply Bolzano–Weierstrass to the first coordinate, then to the second along that subsequence, and so on; closedness keeps the limit in $K$.

**Theorem 6.1.** If $K$ is compact and $f:K\to\mathbb R$ is continuous, then $f$ attains its maximum and minimum on $K$, and $f$ is uniformly continuous on $K$. The proofs of Theorems 5.1 and 5.2 work word for word, with "compact" in place of Bolzano–Weierstrass on $[a,b]$.

This is the existence engine of optimization: **a continuous cost over a compact set of decisions has an optimal decision.** The probability simplex $\Delta_m=\{p\in\mathbb R^m:p_i\ge0,\ \sum_ip_i=1\}$ is compact, so an optimal randomized rule exists (Problem C1).

**Infinite dimensions are different.** In the space of summable sequences with the $\ell_1$ distance, let $e_n$ have a $1$ in place $n$ and $0$ elsewhere. Then $\|e_n-e_m\|_1=2$ for $n\ne m$, so $\{e_n\}$ is closed and bounded but no subsequence converges. Heine–Borel fails beyond $\mathbb R^m$. That is why optimal policies for MDPs with infinitely many states need extra conditions (Module 4.4).

## 7. The Riemann integral and where it breaks

Take a bounded $f$ on $[a,b]$ and a partition $P:\ a=x_0<x_1<\dots<x_k=b$. With $M_i=\sup f$ and $m_i=\inf f$ on $[x_{i-1},x_i]$ and $\Delta x_i=x_i-x_{i-1}$, set
$$U(f,P)=\sum_iM_i\Delta x_i,\qquad L(f,P)=\sum_im_i\Delta x_i .$$
Refining a partition lowers $U$ and raises $L$, and $L(f,P)\le U(f,P')$ for any two partitions. $f$ is **integrable** if $\inf_PU(f,P)=\sup_PL(f,P)$, and the common value is $\int_a^bf$.

**Criterion.** $f$ is integrable if and only if for every $\varepsilon>0$ there is a partition with $U(f,P)-L(f,P)<\varepsilon$ (Abbott Section 7.2).

**Theorem 7.1.** A continuous function on $[a,b]$ is integrable.

*Proof.* By Theorem 5.2 choose $\delta$ with $|x-y|<\delta\Rightarrow|f(x)-f(y)|<\varepsilon/(b-a)$, and a partition with every $\Delta x_i<\delta$. On each piece, $M_i$ and $m_i$ are attained (Theorem 5.1) at two points less than $\delta$ apart, so $M_i-m_i<\varepsilon/(b-a)$. Hence $U-L=\sum_i(M_i-m_i)\Delta x_i<\varepsilon$. $\blacksquare$

**Fundamental theorem of calculus** (Abbott Section 7.5). (i) If $F$ is differentiable on $[a,b]$ and $F'=f$ is integrable, then $\int_a^bf=F(b)-F(a)$. (ii) If $f$ is integrable, then $G(x)=\int_a^xf$ is continuous, and $G'(c)=f(c)$ at every point $c$ where $f$ is continuous.

**Where Riemann breaks.** List the rationals of $[0,1]$ as $q_1,q_2,\dots$ (they are countable, Module 0.1) and let $g_n$ be the indicator of $\{q_1,\dots,q_n\}$. Each $g_n$ is integrable with $\int g_n=0$, since it is $0$ except at finitely many points. The $g_n$ increase to the Dirichlet function $g=\mathbf 1_{\mathbb Q\cap[0,1]}$. But $g$ is not Riemann integrable: every piece of every partition contains rationals and irrationals, so $U(g,P)=1$ and $L(g,P)=0$. An increasing limit of functions with integral $0$ has no integral at all. Probability takes limits like this all the time, so Module 0.5 replaces the Riemann integral with the Lebesgue integral, under which $\int g=0$ and increasing limits behave.

## 8. Uniform convergence: when limits can be swapped

$f_n\to f$ **pointwise** on $A$ if $f_n(x)\to f(x)$ for each $x\in A$; the $N$ may depend on $x$.
$f_n\to f$ **uniformly** on $A$ if $\sup_{x\in A}|f_n(x)-f(x)|\to0$; one $N$ works for every $x$.

For $f_n(x)=x^n$ on $[0,1]$ the pointwise limit is $f=0$ on $[0,1)$ and $f(1)=1$. The convergence is not uniform, because $\sup_{x<1}x^n=1$ for every $n$. On $[0,a]$ with $a<1$ it is uniform: the sup is $a^n\to0$.

**Theorem 8.1 (continuity survives).** A uniform limit of continuous functions is continuous (Problem B6). The example $x^n$ shows that a pointwise limit can break continuity.

**Theorem 8.2 (integrals).** If each $f_n$ is integrable on $[a,b]$ and $f_n\to f$ uniformly, then $f$ is integrable and $\int_a^bf_n\to\int_a^bf$.

*Proof.* Let $\delta_n=\sup_x|f_n(x)-f(x)|\to0$; $f$ is bounded since $|f|\le|f_n|+\delta_n$. Given $\varepsilon>0$, pick $n$ with $\delta_n(b-a)<\varepsilon/3$ and a partition $P$ with $U(f_n,P)-L(f_n,P)<\varepsilon/3$. On every piece $f_n-\delta_n\le f\le f_n+\delta_n$, so $U(f,P)\le U(f_n,P)+\delta_n(b-a)$ and $L(f,P)\ge L(f_n,P)-\delta_n(b-a)$, which gives $U(f,P)-L(f,P)<\varepsilon$. So $f$ is integrable, and integrating $f_n-\delta_n\le f\le f_n+\delta_n$ gives $\big|\int f_n-\int f\big|\le\delta_n(b-a)\to0$. $\blacksquare$

Without uniformity this fails. The tent $h_n$ ($h_n(t)=n^2t$ on $[0,1/n]$, $n^2(2/n-t)$ on $[1/n,2/n]$, $0$ elsewhere on $[0,2]$) has $\int h_n=1$, but $h_n\to0$ at every point (Problem I3). The mass escapes into an ever thinner spike.

**Theorem 8.3 (derivatives).** Suppose each $f_n$ has a continuous derivative on $[a,b]$, $f_n'\to g$ uniformly, and $f_n(a)\to A$. Then $f_n\to f$ uniformly, where $f(x)=A+\int_a^xg$, and $f'=g$.

*Proof.* By the fundamental theorem (i), $f_n(x)=f_n(a)+\int_a^xf_n'$. The limit $g$ is continuous (Theorem 8.1), so $f$ is well defined and $f'=g$ by the fundamental theorem (ii). Finally $|f_n(x)-f(x)|\le|f_n(a)-A|+(b-a)\sup|f_n'-g|\to0$, uniformly in $x$. $\blacksquare$

The hypothesis is on the *derivatives*. The functions $f_n(x)=\sin(nx)/\sqrt n$ converge to $0$ uniformly, but $f_n'(x)=\sqrt n\cos(nx)$ converges at no point at all (at $x=0$, for example, it equals $\sqrt n$). Uniformly close functions can have wildly different slopes. Remember this when you differentiate a smooth surrogate of a simulated cost.

**Series of functions.** Apply Theorems 8.1–8.3 to partial sums. The **Weierstrass M-test**: if $|f_n(x)|\le M_n$ for all $x\in A$ and $\sum M_n<\infty$, then $\sum f_n$ converges uniformly and absolutely on $A$ (Problem B7). For uniformization on $0\le t\le T$, the weights satisfy $0\le e^{-\Lambda t}(\Lambda t)^n/n!\le(\Lambda T)^n/n!$, which is summable, so the series for $p(t)$ converges uniformly on $[0,T]$ and $p$ is continuous. Problem T1 gives the sharper bound you will actually use.

**Power series** $\sum c_nx^n$ converge uniformly on $[-r,r]$ for every $r$ below the radius of convergence, and can be differentiated term by term there (Abbott Section 6.5). This is how generating functions produce moments in Module 1.3.

**Theorem 8.4 (differentiating under the integral sign).** Let $f(x,\theta)$ and $\partial f/\partial\theta$ be continuous on the rectangle $[a,b]\times[c,d]$. Then $J(\theta)=\int_a^bf(x,\theta)\,dx$ is differentiable on $[c,d]$, and
$$J'(\theta)=\int_a^b\frac{\partial f}{\partial\theta}(x,\theta)\,dx .$$

*Proof.* Fix $\theta$ and $\varepsilon>0$. The rectangle is compact (Heine–Borel), so $\partial_\theta f$ is uniformly continuous on it (Theorem 6.1): there is $\delta>0$ with $|\partial_\theta f(x,\theta')-\partial_\theta f(x,\theta)|<\varepsilon$ whenever $|\theta'-\theta|<\delta$, for every $x$. Take $0<|h|<\delta$ with $\theta+h\in[c,d]$. For each $x$, the mean value theorem in $\theta$ gives some $\theta'$ between $\theta$ and $\theta+h$ with $\big(f(x,\theta+h)-f(x,\theta)\big)/h=\partial_\theta f(x,\theta')$. So the continuous function
$$D_h(x)=\frac{f(x,\theta+h)-f(x,\theta)}{h}-\partial_\theta f(x,\theta)$$
satisfies $|D_h(x)|<\varepsilon$ for every $x$. Integrating, $\Big|\frac{J(\theta+h)-J(\theta)}{h}-\int_a^b\partial_\theta f(x,\theta)\,dx\Big|=\Big|\int_a^bD_h\Big|\le\varepsilon(b-a)$. $\blacksquare$

This is the theorem behind pathwise (IPA-type) gradient estimators in Module 3.6: the derivative of an expected cost equals the expected derivative when this kind of uniform control holds. When it fails, as with the moving tent, swapping is wrong and the gradient estimate is biased.

**Double sums.** If every $a_{mn}\ge0$, then $\sum_m\sum_na_{mn}=\sum_n\sum_ma_{mn}$, both sides possibly $+\infty$. The same holds when $\sum_m\sum_n|a_{mn}|<\infty$ (Abbott Section 2.8). Without either condition the order matters. Let $a_{mm}=1$, $a_{m,m+1}=-1$ and all other $a_{mn}=0$. Every row sums to $0$, so $\sum_m\sum_na_{mn}=0$. Column $1$ sums to $1$ and every other column sums to $1-1=0$, so $\sum_n\sum_ma_{mn}=1$. In probability, $E\big[\sum_nX_n\big]=\sum_nE[X_n]$ for nonnegative $X_n$ is the same statement; Module 0.5 proves the integral version.

**Limits inside sums.** If $a_{k,n}\to a_n$ as $k\to\infty$ for each $n$, and $|a_{k,n}|\le b_n$ with $\sum_nb_n<\infty$, then $\sum_na_{k,n}\to\sum_na_n$ (Problem B8). A summable bound that does not depend on $k$ is what licenses the swap. This is the series form of the **dominated convergence theorem** (Module 0.5), the theorem you will use most often in this course.

### I1
Your solver approximates the stand's distribution $p(t)$ by a truncated sum $p_N(t)$. Compare two claims. (P): "for each time $t$, the error goes to $0$ as $N\to\infty$." (U): "for every tolerance there is one $N$ that keeps the error below it at every time of the day." Which is pointwise and which is uniform? Which do you need to print one table of probabilities for the whole day with a guaranteed error? Why does (U) hold for $t\in[0,T]$ but fail for $t\in[0,\infty)$?

#### Hint
In (P), may $N$ depend on $t$? For a fixed $N$, what happens to the error as $t$ grows and the Poisson mean $\Lambda t$ passes $N$?

#### Solution
(P) is pointwise convergence: the $N$ may depend on $t$. (U) is uniform convergence on the set of times. A table for the whole day computed with a single $N$ needs (U); with only (P), the $N$ that worked at 9 am might fail at 6 pm.

Problem T1 shows that the $\ell_1$ error at time $t$ is exactly $P\big(\text{Poisson}(\Lambda t)>N\big)$. This increases with $t$, so on $[0,T]$ the worst time is $t=T$, and one $N$ chosen for $T$ works for the whole horizon. On $[0,\infty)$, for any fixed $N$ the Poisson mean $\Lambda t$ eventually passes $N$ and the error tends to $1$. In Problem D1, $N=190$ gives error $8.5\times10^{-7}$ at $t=60$ but about $1$ at $t=120$. So the convergence is pointwise on $[0,\infty)$, not uniform. Long-horizon codes therefore restart: they use $p(t+s)=p(t)e^{Qs}$ in steps of length $s$.

### I2
A random cost $X$ takes values $x_1,x_2,\dots$ with probabilities $p_1,p_2,\dots$. Why does probability require $\sum_k|x_k|p_k<\infty$ before it defines $E[X]=\sum_kx_kp_k$? Give a distribution for which $\sum_kx_kp_k$ converges in one order but not absolutely.

#### Solution
The list $x_1,x_2,\dots$ is just one way to enumerate the values; listing them differently must not change the mean. If $\sum_k|x_k|p_k<\infty$, every order gives the same sum (Problem B5). (a) Heine–Borel: show bounded, then closed using limits coordinate by coordinate.

#### Solution
(a) *Bounded:* $0\le p_i\le1$ for every $i$. *Closed:* let $p^{(k)}\in\Delta_m$ with $p^{(k)}\to p$ in $\mathbb R^m$. Then each coordinate converges, so $p_i=\lim_kp^{(k)}_i\ge0$ by the order limit theorem, and $\sum_ip_i=\lim_k\sum_ip^{(k)}_i=1$, since a finite sum of limits is the limit of the sums. So $p\in\Delta_m$. By Heine–Borel, $\Delta_m$ is compact.

(b) By Theorem 6.1, the continuous function $c$ attains its minimum on the compact set $\Delta_m$: some $p^*$ has $c(p^*)\le c(p)$ for every randomized rule $p$. (The example cost is a polynomial in $p$, hence continuous.)

(c) *Unbounded:* $f(\theta)=1/(1+\theta)$ on $[0,\infty)$, a waiting penalty that keeps falling as taxis are released faster. Its inf is $0$, never attained. *Not closed:* $f(\theta)=\theta$ on $(0,1]$. Its inf is $0$, approached as $\theta\to0$, but $0$ is not an allowed rate. In practice, missing optima usually mean the decision set needs a bound or should include its boundary.

### C2
Passengers arrive uniformly over an hour, $x\in[0,1]$. With release rate $\theta\in[\theta_{\min},\theta_{\max}]$, $0<\theta_{\min}$, a passenger who arrives at $x$ is still waiting at the end of the hour with probability $e^{-\theta(1-x)}$. So the expected fraction still waiting is $J(\theta)=\int_0^1e^{-\theta(1-x)}\,dx$. (a) Justify differentiating under the integral and write $J'(\theta)$ as an integral. (b) Compute $J$ and $J'$ in closed form, and check that $J'$ really is the derivative of $J$. (c) Give $J(2)$ and $J'(2)$. (d) In one sentence: what does autograd in your code compute, and what does Theorem 8.4 add?

#### Hint
(a) Check the hypotheses of Theorem 8.4. (b) Substitute $u=1-x$ and integrate $ue^{-\theta u}$ by parts.

#### Solution
(a) $f(x,\theta)=e^{-\theta(1-x)}$ and $\partial_\theta f=-(1-x)e^{-\theta(1-x)}$ are continuous on $[0,1]\times[\theta_{\min},\theta_{\max}]$. By Theorem 8.4,
$$J'(\theta)=-\int_0^1(1-x)e^{-\theta(1-x)}\,dx .$$

(b) With $u=1-x$: $J(\theta)=\int_0^1e^{-\theta u}du=\dfrac{1-e^{-\theta}}{\theta}$. By parts, $\int_0^1ue^{-\theta u}du=\Big[-\frac{u}{\theta}e^{-\theta u}\Big]_0^1+\frac1\theta\int_0^1e^{-\theta u}du=\dfrac{1-e^{-\theta}-\theta e^{-\theta}}{\theta^2}$. So
$$J'(\theta)=-\frac{1-(1+\theta)e^{-\theta}}{\theta^2}.$$
Check by differentiating $J$ with the quotient rule: $\dfrac{\theta e^{-\theta}-(1-e^{-\theta})}{\theta^2}=\dfrac{(1+\theta)e^{-\theta}-1}{\theta^2}$, the same.

(c) $J(2)=\frac{1-e^{-2}}2=0.43233$ and $J'(2)=-\frac{1-3e^{-2}}4=-0.14850$. (A central difference with step $10^{-6}$ gives the same to seven digits.) A faster release rate lowers the fraction left waiting, as it should.

(d) Autograd differentiates the computation you wrote, for example a quadrature sum or a truncated series. Theorem 8.4 (with Theorems 8.2–8.3 for limits of such sums) is what guarantees that this derivative converges to the derivative of the true expected cost.

### C3
Drivers decide whether to come to the airport. If a fraction $x$ of the fleet joins, the queue is longer and fewer want to join. A logit choice model gives the equilibrium as a fixed point $x=g(x)$ with $g(x)=\dfrac{1}{1+e^{2x}}$. (a) Show that $g$ maps $[0,1]$ into $[0,1]$ and that $|g'(x)|\le\frac12$. (b) With $x_0=0$ and $x_{k+1}=g(x_k)$, prove that $(x_k)$ converges to a fixed point $x^*$ and that the fixed point is unique. (c) Prove $|x_k-x^*|\le2^{-k}$ and find $k$ that guarantees error at most $10^{-6}$. Compare with the actual error.

#### Hint
$g'=-2g(1-g)$, and $u(1-u)\le\frac14$. Then use the mean value theorem: $|g(a)-g(b)|\le\frac12|a-b|$.

#### Solution
(a) $0<g(x)<1$ for every real $x$. Differentiating, $g'(x)=-\dfrac{2e^{2x}}{(1+e^{2x})^2}=-2g(x)\big(1-g(x)\big)$, and $u(1-u)\le\frac14$ for all $u$, so $|g'|\le\frac12$.

(b) By the mean value theorem, $|g(a)-g(b)|\le\frac12|a-b|$. So $|x_{k+1}-x_k|=|g(x_k)-g(x_{k-1})|\le\frac12|x_k-x_{k-1}|$, and by induction $|x_{k+1}-x_k|\le2^{-k}|x_1-x_0|$. For $m>k$,
$$|x_m-x_k|\le\sum_{j=k}^{m-1}2^{-j}|x_1-x_0|<2^{1-k}|x_1-x_0|\to0,$$
so $(x_k)$ is Cauchy and converges to some $x^*$ (Theorem 2.3). Since $g$ is continuous, $x^*=\lim x_{k+1}=\lim g(x_k)=g(x^*)$. If also $g(y)=y$, then $|x^*-y|=|g(x^*)-g(y)|\le\frac12|x^*-y|$, which forces $y=x^*$.

(c) Let $m\to\infty$ in the display: $|x^*-x_k|\le2^{1-k}|x_1-x_0|=2^{1-k}\cdot\frac12=2^{-k}$, since $x_1=g(0)=\frac12$. This is at most $10^{-6}$ once $2^k\ge10^6$, that is $k\ge20$ ($2^{20}=1{,}048{,}576$).

Numerically $x^*=0.3374158$, and the actual error at $k=20$ is $3.5\times10^{-8}$, about 27 times smaller than the guarantee. The reason is that near $x^*$ the slope is $|g'(x^*)|=2x^*(1-x^*)=0.447$, not $\frac12$. The bound is a guarantee, not an estimate. The same argument, in a space of value functions, proves value iteration converges (Modules 0.7 and 4.2).

### D1
In Python: (a) For $f_n(x)=x^n$, compute $\sup|f_n-f|$ on a grid of $[0,1]$ and on $[0,0.9]$ for $n=10,100,1000,100{,}000$. What does the grid get wrong? (b) Build the taxi stand as a birth–death chain on $z\in\{-5,\dots,40\}$ ($z<0$: $-z$ taxis waiting; $z>0$: $z$ passengers waiting), with taxi arrivals at rate $1$ per minute and passenger arrivals at rate $1.2$ per minute, starting empty. Compute $p(t)$ on $[0,60]$ by uniformization with $N$ chosen from Lemma 4.1 for tolerances $10^{-6}$ and $10^{-10}$, compare with `scipy.linalg.expm`, and check that the error stays below the bound at every time. (c) Keep $N$ fixed and look at $t=120$ and $240$.

#### Hint
Precompute $v_n=p(0)P^n$ once; then $p_N(t)=\sum_{n\le N}w_n(t)v_n$ is one matrix product for all times. Use `scipy.stats.poisson.pmf` for the weights $w_n(t)$.

#### Solution
```python
import math
import numpy as np
from scipy.linalg import expm
from scipy.stats import poisson

# (a) pointwise but not uniform: f_n(x) = x^n on [0,1]
x = np.linspace(0, 1, 10_001)
f = (x == 1.0).astype(float)          # pointwise limit
for n in (10, 100, 1000, 100_000):
    on_01 = np.abs(x**n - f).max()
    on_09 = np.abs(x[x <= 0.9]**n).max()
    print(f"n={n:>6}: grid sup on [0,1] = {on_01:.2e}   sup on [0,0.9] = {on_09:.2e}")

# (b) uniformization for the taxi stand, checked against expm
K, M = 5, 40                  # at most K taxis wait; at most M passengers wait
alpha, beta = 1.0, 1.2        # taxi and passenger arrival rates per minute
states = np.arange(-K, M + 1) # z < 0: -z taxis waiting; z > 0: z passengers waiting
S = len(states)
Q = np.zeros((S, S))
for i, z in enumerate(states):
    if z > -K:
        Q[i, i - 1] = alpha   # a taxi arrives (and is matched if passengers wait)
    if z < M:
        Q[i, i + 1] = beta    # a passenger arrives
    Q[i, i] = -Q[i].sum()
Lam = alpha + beta            # largest total exit rate
P = np.eye(S) + Q / Lam
assert (P >= 0).all() and np.allclose(P.sum(axis=1), 1)
p0 = np.zeros(S); p0[K] = 1.0 # start empty: z = 0
T = 60.0

def tail_bound(lam, N):       # Lemma 4.1; needs N + 2 > lam
    return math.exp(-lam + (N + 1) * math.log(lam) - math.lgamma(N + 2)) / (1 - lam / (N + 2))

def choose_N(tol, lam):
    N = math.ceil(lam)
    while tail_bound(lam, N) > tol:
        N += 1
    return N

ts = np.linspace(0, T, 241)
exact = np.array([p0 @ expm(Q * t) for t in ts])
for tol in (1e-6, 1e-10):
    N = choose_N(tol, Lam * T)
    V = np.zeros((N + 1, S)); V[0] = p0
    for n in range(N):
        V[n + 1] = V[n] @ P               # V[n] = p0 P^n
    W = poisson.pmf(np.arange(N + 1)[None, :], Lam * ts[:, None])
    approx = W @ V
    err = np.abs(approx - exact).sum(axis=1)
    print(f"tol={tol:.0e}: N={N}, max l1 error over [0,{T:.0f}] = {err.max():.2e}, "
          f"at t={ts[err.argmax()]:.1f}; bound = {tail_bound(Lam*T, N):.2e}")

# (c) a fixed N is not uniform on [0, infinity): the error grows with t
N = choose_N(1e-6, Lam * T)
for t in (60, 120, 240):
    print(f"N={N}, t={t}: P(Poisson(Lam t) > N) = {poisson.sf(N, Lam * t):.3e}")
```

### T1
Let the stand have finitely many states and generator $Q$ ($q_{ij}\ge0$ for $i\ne j$, rows summing to $0$). Pick $\Lambda\ge\max_i(-q_{ii})$ and set $P=I+Q/\Lambda$. Take as given (Module 0.3) that $p(t)=p(0)e^{Qt}=\sum_{n\ge0}w_n(t)\,p(0)P^n$ with $w_n(t)=e^{-\Lambda t}(\Lambda t)^n/n!$, and let $p_N(t)$ be the sum up to $n=N$. Prove:
(a) $P$ is a stochastic matrix.
(b) $\|p(t)-p_N(t)\|_1=\sum_{n>N}w_n(t)=P\big(\text{Poisson}(\Lambda t)>N\big)$.
(c) This error increases in $t$, so $\sup_{0\le t\le T}\|p(t)-p_N(t)\|_1=P\big(\text{Poisson}(\Lambda T)>N\big)$: one $N$ serves the whole horizon.
(d) For the stand in D1 ($\Lambda=2.2$ per minute, $T=60$ minutes), use Lemma 4.1 to find $N$ for tolerance $10^{-6}$.

#### Hint
(b) Every vector $p(0)P^n$ is a probability vector, and all terms are nonnegative. (c) Differentiate $F(s)=\sum_{n=0}^Ne^{-s}s^n/n!$ in $s$; the sum telescopes.

#### Solution
(a) Off the diagonal, $P_{ij}=q_{ij}/\Lambda\ge0$. On the diagonal, $P_{ii}=1+q_{ii}/\Lambda\ge0$ because $\Lambda\ge-q_{ii}$. Row sums: $1+\frac1\Lambda\sum_jq_{ij}=1$. So $P$ is stochastic.

(b) If $v$ is a probability (row) vector, $vP$ has nonnegative entries summing to $\sum_iv_i\sum_jP_{ij}=1$. By induction each $v_n=p(0)P^n$ is a probability vector. Then $p(t)-p_N(t)=\sum_{n>N}w_n(t)v_n$, a vector with nonnegative entries. Its $\ell_1$ norm is the sum of its entries, and swapping the order of two sums of nonnegative terms (Section 8, double sums) gives
$$\|p(t)-p_N(t)\|_1=\sum_j\sum_{n>N}w_n(t)(v_n)_j=\sum_{n>N}w_n(t)\sum_j(v_n)_j=\sum_{n>N}w_n(t).$$
Since the $w_n(t)$ are the Poisson($\Lambda t$) probabilities, this is $P(\text{Poisson}(\Lambda t)>N)$. The error equals the bound exactly, which is why D1 matched it to three digits.

(c) Let $F(s)=\sum_{n=0}^Ne^{-s}s^n/n!$, so the error is $1-F(\Lambda t)$. Then
$$F'(s)=\sum_{n=0}^N\Big(-e^{-s}\frac{s^n}{n!}\Big)+\sum_{n=1}^Ne^{-s}\frac{s^{n-1}}{(n-1)!}=-e^{-s}\frac{s^N}{N!}<0\quad(s>0),$$
because the second sum repeats the first except for its last term. So $F$ decreases, the error $1-F(\Lambda t)$ increases in $t$, and its sup over $[0,T]$ is the value at $T$.

(d) $\Lambda T=132$. Lemma 4.1 with $N=189$: $t_{190}=3.947\times10^{-7}$, $r=132/191$, bound $1.278\times10^{-6}$: not enough. With $N=190$: $t_{191}=2.728\times10^{-7}$, $r=132/192=0.6875$, $1/(1-r)=3.2$, bound $8.73\times10^{-7}\le10^{-6}$. So $N=190$, and by (c) it works for every $t\in[0,60]$. The exact error at $t=60$ is $8.53\times10^{-7}$ (Problem D1). (Compute $t_{N+1}$ through logarithms: `math.exp(-lam + (N+1)*math.log(lam) - math.lgamma(N+2))`.)

This is a truncation rule you can state and prove in a paper, rather than a fixed number of terms. Module 3.3 extends it to time-varying rates, where the stand changes with flight banks.
