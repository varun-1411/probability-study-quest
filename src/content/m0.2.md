## The story

Your transient solver computes the distribution of the taxi stand at time $t$ by uniformization:
$$p(t)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}e^{-\Lambda t}\frac{(\Lambda t)^n}{n!}\,p(0)P^n ,$$
and the code stops the sum at some $N$. Three questions follow at once.

1. Does the infinite sum mean anything, and how large is the error from stopping at $N$?
2. Is one $N$ good for every time of the day, or must $N$ grow with $t$?
3. Your optimizer needs the derivative of an expected cost with respect to a release rate or a price $\theta$. May you differentiate inside the sum, or inside an integral over the day?

Real analysis is the subject that answers questions like these, with proofs rather than hopes. Every big theorem in probability (the laws of large numbers, the central limit theorem, monotone and dominated convergence) says that some limit exists, or that two limits can be taken in either order. This module gives you the tools:

- **completeness** (Section 1): limits exist even when you cannot write them down;
- **sequences and series** (Sections 2–4): what "converges" means, and when the order of terms in an infinite sum matters;
- **continuity and compactness** (Sections 5–6): when an optimal decision exists;
- **integration and uniform convergence** (Sections 7–8): when limits, integrals and derivatives can be swapped.

By the end you will be able to answer all three questions about your solver, and Problem T1 does exactly that.

## The picture

Draw these four before you read on.

1. Dots $x_1,x_2,\dots$ on a line, a horizontal band of half-width $\varepsilon$ around a value $x$, and a mark at the index $N$ after which every dot stays inside the band. Shrink $\varepsilon$: the mark moves right. That is convergence.
2. The graphs of $x^n$ on $[0,1]$ for $n=1,2,5,20$. They hug $0$, then shoot up to $1$ at $x=1$. Draw a band of half-width $\tfrac12$ around the limit function ($0$ on $[0,1)$, $1$ at $1$): no graph fits inside it. That is convergence at every point that is **not uniform**.
3. A tent of height $n$ on the base $[0,2/n]$. Its area is $1$ for every $n$, yet at every fixed point it eventually becomes $0$. The limit of the areas ($1$) is not the area of the limit ($0$).
4. The graph of an increasing function on $[a,b]$, cut into four strips. In each strip draw the tallest rectangle that stays under the graph and the shortest one that stays above it. The gap between the two staircases is what must shrink for the integral to exist.

## 1. The real numbers and completeness

### 1.1 Why the rationals are not enough

In computation you never notice the difference between rationals and reals: a computer stores finitely many digits anyway. In proofs about limits the difference is everything. The decimal truncations $1,\ 1.4,\ 1.41,\ 1.414,\dots$ of $\sqrt2$ get closer and closer together, yet no rational number is their limit, because $\sqrt2$ is irrational (Module 0.1). Inside $\mathbb Q$, a sequence can "converge to a hole".

Both $\mathbb Q$ and $\mathbb R$ obey the usual rules of arithmetic and of $<$. What $\mathbb R$ has and $\mathbb Q$ lacks is a single extra axiom, **completeness**, and almost every theorem in this module is a consequence of it.

### 1.2 Upper bounds, sup and inf

A number $b$ is an **upper bound** of a set $A\subseteq\mathbb R$ if $a\le b$ for every $a\in A$. The set is **bounded above** if it has one. A **least upper bound** of $A$ is an upper bound $s$ with $s\le b$ for every upper bound $b$. It is written $\sup A$, the *supremum*.

Examples:

- $A=[0,1)$. The upper bounds are exactly the numbers $\ge1$, so $\sup A=1$. But $1\notin A$, so $A$ has **no maximum**: whatever $a<1$ you pick, $(a+1)/2$ is a bigger element.
- $A=\{1-1/n:n\in\mathbb N\}=\{0,\tfrac12,\tfrac23,\tfrac34,\dots\}$. Again $\sup A=1$, not attained.
- $A=\mathbb N$. No upper bound at all.
- $A=\varnothing$. Every real number is an upper bound, so there is no least one. This is why the axiom below insists on *nonempty* sets.

If a set has a maximum, the maximum is its sup. The sup is the right replacement for the maximum when the maximum may not exist.

**Axiom of completeness.** Every nonempty set $A\subseteq\mathbb R$ that is bounded above has a least upper bound $\sup A$ in $\mathbb R$.

Likewise every nonempty set bounded below has a greatest lower bound, the *infimum* $\inf A=-\sup(-A)$.

The axiom fails in $\mathbb Q$: the set $\{x\in\mathbb Q:x^2<2\}$ is bounded above, but its least upper bound, $\sqrt2$, is not rational.

**Lemma 1.1 (how to use a sup).** Let $s$ be an upper bound of $A$. Then $s=\sup A$ if and only if for every $\varepsilon>0$ there is $a\in A$ with $a>s-\varepsilon$.

In words: the sup can be approached from below by elements of the set, as closely as you like. You will use this lemma in almost every proof about sups.

*Plan.* "Least" means anything smaller is not an upper bound; unpack that.

*Proof.* If $s=\sup A$ and $\varepsilon>0$, then $s-\varepsilon$ is smaller than the least upper bound, so it is not an upper bound: some $a\in A$ exceeds it. Conversely, suppose the condition holds and $b<s$ is another upper bound. Take $\varepsilon=s-b$: some $a\in A$ has $a>s-\varepsilon=b$, contradicting that $b$ is an upper bound. So no upper bound is smaller than $s$. $\blacksquare$

*Worked example.* Suppose taxis arrive $t$ times as fast as passengers, and the long-run fraction of passengers who find a taxi waiting is $f(t)=t/(1+t)$ (a toy formula). Claim: $\sup\{f(t):t\ge0\}=1$, and no $t$ attains it. First, $f(t)<1$ for every $t$, so $1$ is an upper bound and is never reached. Given $\varepsilon\in(0,1)$, we want $t/(1+t)>1-\varepsilon$. Rearranging, this holds when $t>(1-\varepsilon)/\varepsilon$, so such a $t$ exists, and Lemma 1.1 gives $\sup=1$. In words: you can serve as close to 100% of passengers as you like by sending more taxis, but never all of them.

*Common mistakes.* (1) Thinking $\sup A$ must belong to $A$. (2) Checking only that $s$ is an upper bound and forgetting "least"; Lemma 1.1 is the second half. (3) Writing $\max A$ before you have shown a maximum exists.

### 1.3 Consequences: the Archimedean property and density

**Archimedean property.** $\mathbb N$ is not bounded above; equivalently, for every $\varepsilon>0$ there is $n\in\mathbb N$ with $1/n<\varepsilon$ (Problem B1 proves it from completeness). It says there are no "infinitely small" positive real numbers. You use it every time you write "choose $N>1/\varepsilon$".

**Density of $\mathbb Q$.** Between any two reals $a<b$ there is a rational.

*Proof.* Choose $n$ with $1/n<b-a$. Let $m$ be the smallest integer with $m>na$ (it exists by the Archimedean property and well-ordering). Then $m-1\le na<m$, so $a<\frac mn\le a+\frac1n<b$. $\blacksquare$

Density is why, in Module 0.1, checking a right-continuous queue path at rational times was enough: every interval of time contains rational times.

**Quick checks.**

1. Find $\sup$ and $\inf$ of $(0,1]\cup\{2\}$, and say whether each is attained.
2. True or false: every nonempty bounded-above subset of $\mathbb Q$ has a sup that lies in $\mathbb Q$.
3. If $s=\sup A$ and $s\notin A$, explain why $A$ must be infinite.

*Answers.* (1) $\sup=2$, attained (it is the maximum); $\inf=0$, not attained. (2) False: $\{x\in\mathbb Q:x^2<2\}$ has sup $\sqrt2\notin\mathbb Q$. (3) A finite nonempty set has a maximum, and the maximum is the sup, which would then be in $A$.

## 2. Sequences

### 2.1 What convergence means

**Definition.** $x_n\to x$ if for every $\varepsilon>0$ there is $N$ such that $|x_n-x|<\varepsilon$ for all $n\ge N$.

Read it as the game from Module 0.1: an opponent names a tolerance $\varepsilon$; you answer with a starting index $N$, which may depend on $\varepsilon$; from then on, every term must be within $\varepsilon$ of $x$. Convergence means you can always answer.

*Worked example 1.* $1/n\to0$. Given $\varepsilon>0$, choose $N>1/\varepsilon$. For $n\ge N$, $|1/n-0|=1/n\le1/N<\varepsilon$.

*Worked example 2: how to find $N$.* Show $\frac{2n+1}{n+3}\to2$. **Scratch work** (not part of the proof): $\left|\frac{2n+1}{n+3}-2\right|=\frac{|2n+1-2n-6|}{n+3}=\frac5{n+3}<\frac5n$, and $\frac5n<\varepsilon$ when $n>5/\varepsilon$. **Proof** (written forwards): given $\varepsilon>0$, choose $N>5/\varepsilon$; for $n\ge N$, $\left|\frac{2n+1}{n+3}-2\right|=\frac5{n+3}<\frac5n\le\frac5N<\varepsilon$. You find $N$ by working backwards from the inequality you want, then present the argument forwards. Simplifying with a crude bound such as $\frac5{n+3}<\frac5n$ is allowed and saves effort; you never need the smallest $N$.

*Worked example 3: proving divergence.* $x_n=(-1)^n$ does not converge. Suppose $x_n\to x$. With $\varepsilon=1$ there is $N$ such that all later terms are within $1$ of $x$. Beyond $N$ there is a term equal to $1$ and a term equal to $-1$, so $2=|1-(-1)|\le|1-x|+|x-(-1)|<1+1=2$, a contradiction.

*Common mistakes.* (1) Letting $N$ depend on $n$; it may depend only on $\varepsilon$. (2) Checking one value of $\varepsilon$, say $0.01$; the definition needs every $\varepsilon>0$. (3) Thinking "the terms get closer to $x$" means convergence to $x$: the terms of $1/n$ get closer to $-1$ at every step, yet $1/n$ does not converge to $-1$.

### 2.2 The limit laws

These facts are proved in Abbott Section 2.3; two sample proofs show the techniques.

- **Uniqueness.** If $x_n\to x$ and $x_n\to y$, then for every $\varepsilon>0$, choosing $n$ large, $|x-y|\le|x-x_n|+|x_n-y|<2\varepsilon$. A nonnegative number smaller than every positive number is $0$, so $x=y$.
- **Convergent sequences are bounded.** With $\varepsilon=1$ there is $N$ with $|x_n|<|x|+1$ for $n\ge N$, so $|x_n|\le\max\{|x_1|,\dots,|x_{N-1}|,|x|+1\}$ for every $n$.
- **Algebraic limit theorem.** If $x_n\to x$ and $y_n\to y$, then $x_n+y_n\to x+y$, $x_ny_n\to xy$, and $x_n/y_n\to x/y$ when $y\ne0$. *Sum, as a sample:* given $\varepsilon$, choose $N$ so that $|x_n-x|<\varepsilon/2$ and $|y_n-y|<\varepsilon/2$ for $n\ge N$; then $|(x_n+y_n)-(x+y)|<\varepsilon$. This "$\varepsilon/2$ trick" is everywhere. For products, write $x_ny_n-xy=x_n(y_n-y)+y(x_n-x)$ and use that $(x_n)$ is bounded.
- **Order limit theorem.** If $x_n\le y_n$ for all $n$, then $\lim x_n\le\lim y_n$. Strict inequalities do **not** survive: $1/n>0$ for every $n$, but the limit is $0$.
- **Squeeze.** If $x_n\le z_n\le y_n$ and $x_n,y_n\to L$, then $z_n\to L$.

### 2.3 Monotone sequences

**Theorem 2.1 (monotone convergence).** An increasing sequence that is bounded above converges, and its limit is $\sup_n x_n$.

*Plan.* The only candidate for the limit is the sup; use Lemma 1.1 to get one term close to it, and monotonicity to keep all later terms close.

*Proof.* Let $s=\sup_n x_n$, which exists by completeness. Given $\varepsilon>0$, Lemma 1.1 gives $N$ with $x_N>s-\varepsilon$. For $n\ge N$, $s-\varepsilon<x_N\le x_n\le s$. $\blacksquare$

This gives a limit without telling you what it is. *Example:* $s_n=1+\frac1{2^2}+\dots+\frac1{n^2}$ increases, and it stays below $2$ (Problem A4(a)), so it converges. Its limit happens to be $\pi^2/6$, but the theorem does not need to know that. *At the stand:* the probability that the queue has exceeded 20 by day $n$ increases with $n$ and is at most $1$, so it converges. In Module 1.2 this becomes continuity of probability along increasing events, and later the monotone convergence theorem for integrals.

### 2.4 Subsequences and Bolzano–Weierstrass

A **subsequence** of $(x_n)$ keeps infinitely many terms in their original order: $x_{n_1},x_{n_2},\dots$ with $n_1<n_2<\cdots$. If $x_n\to x$, every subsequence also converges to $x$. So a quick way to show divergence is to find two subsequences with different limits, as with the even and odd terms of $(-1)^n$.

**Theorem 2.2 (Bolzano–Weierstrass).** Every bounded sequence has a convergent subsequence.

*Plan.* Extract a monotone subsequence, then apply Theorem 2.1.

*Proof.* Call an index $m$ a *peak* if $x_m\ge x_n$ for every $n>m$: from $x_m$, the view to the right is all downhill. If there are infinitely many peaks $m_1<m_2<\cdots$, then $x_{m_1}\ge x_{m_2}\ge\cdots$ is a decreasing subsequence. If there are finitely many, pick $n_1$ beyond the last one. Since $n_1$ is not a peak, some $n_2>n_1$ has $x_{n_2}>x_{n_1}$; $n_2$ is not a peak either, so some $n_3>n_2$ has $x_{n_3}>x_{n_2}$; and so on. This builds an increasing subsequence. Either way the subsequence is monotone and bounded, so it converges by Theorem 2.1 (for the decreasing case apply the theorem to $-x_n$). $\blacksquare$

Bolzano–Weierstrass is the engine behind every existence result in Sections 5 and 6: to show a minimizer exists, take a sequence of better and better decisions and extract a convergent subsequence.

### 2.5 Cauchy sequences

$(x_n)$ is **Cauchy** if for every $\varepsilon>0$ there is $N$ with $|x_m-x_n|<\varepsilon$ for all $m,n\ge N$. The terms get close to *each other*; no limit is mentioned.

**Theorem 2.3.** A sequence of real numbers converges if and only if it is Cauchy (Problem B3).

This is the test you use when you cannot name the limit: value iteration, a fixed-point solver (Problem C3), the partial sums of a series. *Example:* the partial sums $s_n=\sum_{k\le n}1/k^2$ are Cauchy, because for $m>n$, using $\frac1{k^2}\le\frac1{k-1}-\frac1k$,
$$s_m-s_n=\sum_{k=n+1}^m\frac1{k^2}\le\frac1n-\frac1m<\frac1n .$$
*A non-example:* the harmonic partial sums $h_n=1+\frac12+\dots+\frac1n$ are not Cauchy, since $h_{2n}-h_n$ has $n$ terms, each at least $\frac1{2n}$, so $h_{2n}-h_n\ge\frac12$ for every $n$.

**A warning for your code.** "Stop when successive iterates differ by less than $10^{-8}$" does **not** test the Cauchy condition. The harmonic sums have $h_{n+1}-h_n=\frac1{n+1}\to0$, yet they diverge. Small steps prove nothing by themselves. What turns small steps into a guaranteed error is a *rate*: if each step is at most a fixed fraction $\rho<1$ of the previous one, the remaining distance is at most $\frac{\rho}{1-\rho}$ times the last step. Problem C3 works this out.

The theorem fails in $\mathbb Q$ (the truncations of $\sqrt2$ are Cauchy with no rational limit). "Cauchy sequences converge" is exactly what completeness means, and Section 6 uses it as the definition of completeness in other spaces.

**Quick checks.**

1. Prove $1/\sqrt n\to0$.
2. Give a sequence with $x_{n+1}-x_n\to0$ that does not converge.
3. If $x_n\to x$, does $x_{2n}\to x$?

*Answers.* (1) Given $\varepsilon$, take $N>1/\varepsilon^2$; for $n\ge N$, $1/\sqrt n\le1/\sqrt N<\varepsilon$. (2) The harmonic sums $h_n$, or $\sqrt n$. (3) Yes: it is a subsequence.

## 3. limsup and liminf

### 3.1 Long-run bounds for sequences that never settle

Many sequences in probability never converge but still have useful long-run bounds. Let $f_n$ be the fraction of hours with blocking during the first $n$ days. It may oscillate forever with the flight timetable. What you can still say is something like "in the long run, it is never above 5%", and that is a statement about the **limsup**.

Define the sup and inf of the **tail** from $n$ on:
$$y_n=\sup_{k\ge n}x_k,\qquad z_n=\inf_{k\ge n}x_k .$$
As $n$ grows you take the sup over fewer terms, so $y_n$ can only go down, and $z_n$ can only go up. By Theorem 2.1 both have limits in $[-\infty,\infty]$:
$$\limsup_nx_n=\lim_ny_n=\inf_n\sup_{k\ge n}x_k,\qquad\liminf_nx_n=\lim_nz_n=\sup_n\inf_{k\ge n}x_k .$$
Picture the graph of the sequence with an upper envelope sliding down and a lower envelope sliding up; limsup and liminf are where the envelopes end up.

*Worked example.* $x_n=(-1)^n+\frac1n$:

| $n$ | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 | 6 | 7 | 8 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| $x_n$ | $0$ | $1.5$ | $-0.667$ | $1.25$ | $-0.8$ | $1.167$ | $-0.857$ | $1.125$ |
| $y_n$ | $1.5$ | $1.5$ | $1.25$ | $1.25$ | $1.167$ | $1.167$ | $1.125$ | $1.125$ |

The even terms $1+\frac1n$ decrease toward $1$, so $y_n$ is the first even term at or after $n$, and $y_n\to1$. The odd terms $-1+\frac1n$ decrease toward $-1$ without reaching it, so $z_n=-1$ for every $n$ (an inf that is never attained). Hence $\limsup x_n=1$ and $\liminf x_n=-1$.

### 3.2 The facts you will use

- $\liminf x_n\le\limsup x_n$, because $z_n\le y_n$.
- $x_n\to L$ if and only if $\liminf x_n=\limsup x_n=L$ (Problem B4). So limsup and liminf are a two-sided test for convergence.
- If $L=\limsup x_n$ is finite, then for every $\varepsilon>0$: $x_n<L+\varepsilon$ for all large $n$, and $x_n>L-\varepsilon$ for infinitely many $n$. So $\limsup$ is the largest limit of any subsequence, and $\liminf$ the smallest.
- The values $\pm\infty$ are allowed. For $x_n=n$, both are $+\infty$. For $x_n=n$ on even $n$ and $0$ on odd $n$, $\limsup=+\infty$ and $\liminf=0$.

*At the stand.* "$\limsup_nf_n\le0.05$" says: for any margin, say $0.01$, the blocking fraction is below $6\%$ from some day on. It does not say $f_n$ settles down.

### 3.3 Back to events

For events $A_n$, the indicators $\mathbf 1_{A_n}(\omega)$ form a $0$–$1$ sequence for each outcome $\omega$. Its limsup is $1$ exactly when it equals $1$ infinitely often, that is, when $\omega\in\limsup_nA_n$; its liminf is $1$ exactly when it is eventually always $1$. So
$$\mathbf 1_{\limsup_nA_n}=\limsup_n\mathbf 1_{A_n},\qquad\mathbf 1_{\liminf_nA_n}=\liminf_n\mathbf 1_{A_n}.$$
The set operations from Module 0.1 are the number operations applied to indicators, which is why they share their names.

**Quick checks.**

1. Find $\limsup$ and $\liminf$ of $1/n$.
2. Find them for $x_n=\sin(n\pi/2)$.
3. A sequence has $\limsup=3$. Must some term equal $3$? Must infinitely many terms exceed $2.9$?

*Answers.* (1) Both $0$; the sequence converges. (2) The values cycle $1,0,-1,0$, so $\limsup=1$, $\liminf=-1$. (3) No (take $x_n=3-\frac1n$). Yes.

## 4. Series

### 4.1 What an infinite sum means

An infinite sum $\sum_{n\ge1}a_n$ is defined as the limit of its **partial sums** $s_n=a_1+\dots+a_n$. It **converges** if $(s_n)$ converges; otherwise it diverges. There is no way to "add infinitely many numbers" other than this.

Applying Theorem 2.3 to the partial sums gives the **Cauchy criterion**: $\sum a_n$ converges if and only if for every $\varepsilon>0$ there is $N$ with
$$|a_{m+1}+a_{m+2}+\dots+a_n|<\varepsilon\qquad\text{whenever }n>m\ge N .$$
In words: blocks of terms far out add up to almost nothing.

**Terms must go to zero.** Taking $n=m+1$ in the criterion shows that if $\sum a_n$ converges then $a_n\to0$. The converse is false, and the standard counterexample is worth remembering. In the harmonic series
$$1+\tfrac12+\big(\tfrac13+\tfrac14\big)+\big(\tfrac15+\tfrac16+\tfrac17+\tfrac18\big)+\cdots$$
each bracket contains $2^{k}$ terms, each at least $2^{-(k+1)}$, so each bracket is at least $\tfrac12$. Infinitely many halves make the sum infinite, even though the terms go to $0$.

### 4.2 Series with nonnegative terms

If every $a_n\ge0$, the partial sums increase, so by Theorem 2.1 the series converges exactly when the partial sums are bounded. This gives the most useful test:

- **Comparison.** If $0\le a_n\le b_n$ and $\sum b_n$ converges, so does $\sum a_n$ (its partial sums are bounded by $\sum b_n$). If $\sum a_n$ diverges, so does $\sum b_n$.
- **Geometric series.** For $r\ne1$, $1+r+\dots+r^n=\frac{1-r^{n+1}}{1-r}$ (multiply out $(1-r)(1+\dots+r^n)$). If $|r|<1$, $r^{n+1}\to0$, so $\sum_{n\ge0}r^n=\frac1{1-r}$. If $|r|\ge1$ the terms do not go to $0$ and the series diverges.
- **$p$-series.** $\sum1/n^p$ converges if $p>1$ and diverges if $p\le1$ (Abbott Section 2.4).

*At the stand.* In the simplest queue (Module 3.4), the long-run probability of $k$ waiting passengers is $\pi_k=(1-\rho)\rho^k$, $k=0,1,2,\dots$, where $\rho<1$ is the ratio of passenger to taxi arrival rates. These are probabilities because $\sum_k(1-\rho)\rho^k=(1-\rho)\cdot\frac1{1-\rho}=1$, a geometric series. Section 8.5 computes the mean queue length from the same series.

### 4.3 Absolute convergence and the ratio test

$\sum a_n$ **converges absolutely** if $\sum|a_n|<\infty$. Absolute convergence implies convergence: by the triangle inequality, $|a_{m+1}+\dots+a_n|\le|a_{m+1}|+\dots+|a_n|$, so the Cauchy criterion for $\sum|a_n|$ gives it for $\sum a_n$. A series that converges but not absolutely converges **conditionally**; the alternating harmonic series $1-\tfrac12+\tfrac13-\tfrac14+\cdots$ is the standard example (it converges, to $\ln2$, by the alternating series test in Abbott Section 2.7).

**Ratio test.** If $|a_{n+1}/a_n|\to\rho<1$, the series converges absolutely; if $\rho>1$, it diverges; if $\rho=1$, the test says nothing.

*Proof for $\rho<1$.* Choose $r$ with $\rho<r<1$. For $n\ge$ some $N$, $|a_{n+1}|\le r|a_n|$, so $|a_n|\le|a_N|r^{n-N}$: the tail is dominated by a convergent geometric series. (For $\rho>1$ the terms eventually grow, so they do not go to $0$.) $\blacksquare$

The case $\rho=1$ really is undecided: $\sum1/n$ diverges and $\sum1/n^2$ converges, and both have ratio tending to $1$.

### 4.4 When the order of terms matters

For finite sums the order never matters. For infinite sums it can. Take the alternating harmonic series, which sums to $\ln2$, and reorder it as one positive term followed by two negative ones:
$$1-\tfrac12-\tfrac14+\tfrac13-\tfrac16-\tfrac18+\tfrac15-\tfrac1{10}-\tfrac1{12}+\cdots$$
Every term of the original series appears exactly once. But grouping in threes, each group is $\frac1{2k-1}-\frac1{2(2k-1)}-\frac1{4k}=\frac12\Big(\frac1{2k-1}-\frac1{2k}\Big)$, so the rearranged series sums to $\frac12\ln2$: half the original. **Riemann's rearrangement theorem** says more: a conditionally convergent series can be reordered to sum to *any* number, or to diverge.

Absolute convergence removes the problem entirely: an absolutely convergent series gives the same sum in every order (Problem B5).

This is why $E[X]=\sum_kx_k\,p_k$ for a discrete random variable is defined only when $\sum_k|x_k|\,p_k<\infty$. The values of $X$ come in no natural order, so the expectation must not depend on one (Problem I2).

### 4.5 The Poisson series and its tail

$\sum_{n\ge0}\lambda^n/n!=e^\lambda$ for every real $\lambda$ (Problem B8 proves this from $(1+\lambda/k)^k\to e^\lambda$). So for $\lambda>0$ the numbers $e^{-\lambda}\lambda^n/n!$ are probabilities: the Poisson($\lambda$) distribution, the number of arrivals in a period with mean $\lambda$ arrivals. Your uniformization weights are exactly these, with $\lambda=\Lambda t$.

How much probability lies beyond $N$? The terms $t_n=e^{-\lambda}\lambda^n/n!$ grow while $n+1<\lambda$ and shrink once $n+1>\lambda$, because $t_{n+1}/t_n=\lambda/(n+1)$. Past $\lambda$ they shrink at least as fast as a geometric series, which we can sum.

**Lemma 4.1 (Poisson tail bound).** Let $\lambda>0$ and let $N$ be an integer with $N+2>\lambda$. Put $r=\lambda/(N+2)$. Then
$$\sum_{n=N+1}^{\infty}e^{-\lambda}\frac{\lambda^n}{n!}\;\le\;e^{-\lambda}\frac{\lambda^{N+1}}{(N+1)!}\cdot\frac{1}{1-r}.$$

*Plan.* Bound each ratio of consecutive terms by $r$, then compare with a geometric series.

*Proof.* For $n\ge N+1$, $t_{n+1}/t_n=\lambda/(n+1)\le\lambda/(N+2)=r<1$. By induction, $t_n\le t_{N+1}r^{\,n-N-1}$ for $n\ge N+1$. Summing the geometric series, $\sum_{n\ge N+1}t_n\le t_{N+1}\sum_{j\ge0}r^j=t_{N+1}/(1-r)$. $\blacksquare$

*Worked example.* $\lambda=1$, $N=5$: $t_6=e^{-1}/720=5.11\times10^{-4}$ and $r=\frac17$, so the bound is $5.11\times10^{-4}\times\frac76=5.96\times10^{-4}$. The exact tail is $5.94\times10^{-4}$. The bound is within half a percent, because past $\lambda$ the first omitted term dominates the tail.

This is the bound behind the truncation level $N$ in your uniformization code (Problems A6, T1 and D1).

**Quick checks.**

1. Does $\sum\frac1{n^2+n}$ converge?
2. Does $\sum(-1)^n$ converge?
3. Does $\sum\frac{n^{10}}{2^n}$ converge?

*Answers.* (1) Yes, by comparison with $\sum1/n^2$ (in fact it telescopes to $1$). (2) No: the terms do not go to $0$. (3) Yes, by the ratio test: $\frac{(n+1)^{10}}{2n^{10}}\to\frac12$.

## 5. Continuous functions

### 5.1 The definition

$f:A\to\mathbb R$ is **continuous at** $c\in A$ if for every $\varepsilon>0$ there is $\delta>0$ such that $x\in A$ and $|x-c|<\delta$ give $|f(x)-f(c)|<\varepsilon$. It is **continuous** if it is continuous at every point of $A$.

As a game: the opponent names an output tolerance $\varepsilon$; you answer with an input tolerance $\delta$; every input within $\delta$ of $c$ must give an output within $\varepsilon$ of $f(c)$. Small changes in the input cause small changes in the output.

*Worked example.* $f(x)=x^2$ is continuous at every $c$. Scratch work: $|x^2-c^2|=|x-c|\,|x+c|$. If $|x-c|<1$ then $|x|<|c|+1$, so $|x+c|<2|c|+1$. Proof: given $\varepsilon$, take $\delta=\min\big(1,\frac{\varepsilon}{2|c|+1}\big)$. If $|x-c|<\delta$, then $|x^2-c^2|<\delta(2|c|+1)\le\varepsilon$. Notice that $\delta$ depends on $c$: the graph is steeper far from $0$. Section 5.5 comes back to this.

*A discontinuity you will meet.* The distribution function of a constant random variable $X\equiv0$ is $F(x)=P(X\le x)=\mathbf 1_{[0,\infty)}(x)$. It jumps at $0$: with $\varepsilon=\frac12$, no $\delta$ works, because points just left of $0$ have $F=0$ while $F(0)=1$. It is, however, *right-continuous*; every distribution function is (Module 1.2).

### 5.2 The sequential form

**Theorem.** $f$ is continuous at $c$ if and only if $f(x_n)\to f(c)$ for every sequence $x_n\to c$ in $A$.

*Proof.* ($\Rightarrow$) Given $\varepsilon$, take the $\delta$ from continuity, then $N$ with $|x_n-c|<\delta$ for $n\ge N$; for those $n$, $|f(x_n)-f(c)|<\varepsilon$. ($\Leftarrow$, by contrapositive) If $f$ is not continuous at $c$, some $\varepsilon$ defeats every $\delta$; taking $\delta=1/n$ gives points $x_n$ with $|x_n-c|<1/n$ but $|f(x_n)-f(c)|\ge\varepsilon$. Then $x_n\to c$ while $f(x_n)\not\to f(c)$. $\blacksquare$

This is the version you will use most, because it turns continuity questions into sequence questions, where Section 2's tools apply. For instance, combined with the limit laws it shows that sums, products, quotients (where defined) and compositions of continuous functions are continuous. So polynomials, $e^x$, and any cost formula built from them are continuous.

### 5.3 The extreme value theorem

**Theorem 5.1 (extreme value theorem).** A continuous function on $[a,b]$ is bounded and attains its maximum and its minimum.

*Plan.* Take a sequence of points where $f$ gets close to its sup; Bolzano–Weierstrass gives a convergent subsequence; continuity carries the values along.

*Proof.* *Bounded:* if not, pick $x_n\in[a,b]$ with $|f(x_n)|>n$. By Bolzano–Weierstrass a subsequence $x_{n_k}\to c$, and $c\in[a,b]$ because $a\le x_{n_k}\le b$. Continuity gives $f(x_{n_k})\to f(c)$; a convergent sequence is bounded, but $|f(x_{n_k})|>n_k\to\infty$. *Maximum:* let $M=\sup f([a,b])$, which is finite. By Lemma 1.1 pick $x_n$ with $f(x_n)>M-1/n$. A subsequence converges to some $c\in[a,b]$, and from $M-1/n_k<f(x_{n_k})\le M$ and $f(x_{n_k})\to f(c)$ we get $f(c)=M$. *Minimum:* apply this to $-f$. $\blacksquare$

Every hypothesis matters:

- **Closed interval:** $f(x)=1/x$ on $(0,1]$ is continuous and unbounded; $f(x)=x$ on $(0,1)$ has no maximum.
- **Bounded interval:** $f(x)=x$ on $[0,\infty)$ is unbounded.
- **Continuity:** $f(x)=x$ for $x<1$ and $f(1)=0$, on $[0,1]$, has $\sup=1$ but never reaches it.

### 5.4 The intermediate value theorem

**Theorem.** If $f$ is continuous on $[a,b]$ and $y$ lies between $f(a)$ and $f(b)$, then $f(c)=y$ for some $c\in[a,b]$.

*Proof* (for $f(a)<y<f(b)$; the other case is the same with $-f$). Let $S=\{x\in[a,b]:f(x)\le y\}$, which contains $a$ and is bounded, and let $c=\sup S$. If $f(c)<y$, continuity gives $\delta>0$ with $f<y$ on $[c,c+\delta)$; since $f(b)>y$, $c<b$, so some points just above $c$ lie in $S$, contradicting that $c$ is an upper bound. If $f(c)>y$, continuity gives $\delta$ with $f>y$ on $(c-\delta,c]$, so no point of $S$ lies in $(c-\delta,c]$ and $c-\delta$ is a smaller upper bound, contradicting "least". So $f(c)=y$. $\blacksquare$

*At the stand.* If expected demand $D(\theta)$ at surge price $\theta$ is continuous, with $D(0)$ above the number of available taxis and $D(\theta_{\max})$ below it, then some price in between makes demand match supply exactly. The same argument shows that a continuous distribution function takes every value in $(0,1)$, so its quantiles exist.

### 5.5 Uniform continuity

In the $x^2$ example, $\delta$ had to shrink as $c$ grew. $f$ is **uniformly continuous** on $A$ if one $\delta$ works at every point: for every $\varepsilon>0$ there is $\delta>0$ such that $x,y\in A$ and $|x-y|<\delta$ give $|f(x)-f(y)|<\varepsilon$. In the game, you must name $\delta$ before you know where in $A$ the opponent will test it.

- $x^2$ is **not** uniformly continuous on $\mathbb R$: with $x=n$ and $y=n+\frac1n$, $|x-y|\to0$ but $y^2-x^2=2+\frac1{n^2}\ge2$.
- $1/x$ is not uniformly continuous on $(0,1]$: near $0$ it is arbitrarily steep.
- A **Lipschitz** function, $|f(x)-f(y)|\le L|x-y|$, is uniformly continuous: take $\delta=\varepsilon/L$.

**Theorem 5.2.** A continuous function on $[a,b]$ is uniformly continuous.

*Plan.* Suppose not, collect bad pairs of points, and use Bolzano–Weierstrass to make them pile up at one point where continuity fails.

*Proof.* If not, there are $\varepsilon>0$ and points $x_n,y_n\in[a,b]$ with $|x_n-y_n|<1/n$ but $|f(x_n)-f(y_n)|\ge\varepsilon$. A subsequence $x_{n_k}\to c\in[a,b]$, and then $y_{n_k}\to c$ too. By continuity at $c$, $f(x_{n_k})-f(y_{n_k})\to f(c)-f(c)=0$, a contradiction. $\blacksquare$

Uniform continuity is what makes continuous functions integrable (Theorem 7.1) and what lets you differentiate under an integral (Theorem 8.4).

### 5.6 The mean value theorem

**Mean value theorem.** If $f$ is continuous on $[a,b]$ and differentiable on $(a,b)$, then $f(b)-f(a)=f'(\xi)(b-a)$ for some $\xi\in(a,b)$ (Abbott Section 5.3). Picture: somewhere, the tangent is parallel to the chord.

Consequences you will use:

- If $f'=0$ on an interval, $f$ is constant there.
- If $|f'|\le L$ on an interval, then $|f(x)-f(y)|\le L|x-y|$ there. For example $|\sin x-\sin y|\le|x-y|$.
- *Sensitivity at the stand:* if an expected cost $J(\theta)$ has $|J'(\theta)|\le L$ for all rates in a range, then mis-estimating the rate by $\delta$ changes the cost by at most $L\delta$. Problem C3 uses the same idea to prove that an iteration contracts.

**Quick checks.**

1. Is $\sqrt x$ uniformly continuous on $[0,1]$?
2. Give a continuous function on $(0,1)$ with no maximum.
3. Show that $x^3+x-1$ has a root in $[0,1]$.

*Answers.* (1) Yes, by Theorem 5.2, even though its derivative is unbounded near $0$: uniform continuity does not need a Lipschitz bound. (2) $f(x)=x$. (3) It is continuous with $f(0)=-1<0<1=f(1)$; apply the intermediate value theorem.

## 6. Metric spaces and compactness

### 6.1 Why generalize

So far, distance has meant $|x-y|$ between numbers. Later you will need distances between other objects: between two probability distributions of the queue, between two functions of time, between two value functions in an MDP. The definitions of convergence, Cauchy sequence and compactness only ever used three properties of $|x-y|$, so they carry over to any notion of distance with those properties. One set of theorems then serves every space.

### 6.2 Metrics

A **metric** on a set $X$ is a function $d$ with, for all $x,y,z\in X$:

- $d(x,y)\ge0$, with equality only when $x=y$;
- $d(x,y)=d(y,x)$;
- the triangle inequality $d(x,z)\le d(x,y)+d(y,z)$.

Examples:

- **$\mathbb R^m$** with the Euclidean distance, or with $\|x-y\|_1=\sum_i|x_i-y_i|$. The triangle inequality for $\|\cdot\|_1$ follows from $|a+b|\le|a|+|b|$ in each coordinate.
- **Probability vectors with total variation.** For two distributions $p,q$ on states $1,\dots,m$, the **total variation distance** is half the $\ell_1$ distance, and it equals the largest difference the two distributions assign to any event: $\tfrac12\|p-q\|_1=\max_A|p(A)-q(A)|$. Example: $p=(0.5,0.3,0.2)$ and $q=(0.4,0.4,0.2)$ have $\|p-q\|_1=0.2$, so total variation $0.1$, achieved by the event $A=\{1\}$. *At the stand:* if two models of the 6 pm queue are within $0.01$ in total variation, then no statement about the queue gets probabilities differing by more than $0.01$.
- **Bounded functions** on a set $A$ with $d_\infty(f,g)=\sup_{x\in A}|f(x)-g(x)|$, the largest vertical gap between the graphs. The triangle inequality holds because the sup of a sum is at most the sum of the sups. Convergence in $d_\infty$ is uniform convergence (Section 8).

### 6.3 Convergence, closed sets and completeness

Everything from Section 2 carries over with $d(x,y)$ in place of $|x-y|$: $x_n\to x$ means $d(x_n,x)\to0$, and Cauchy means $d(x_m,x_n)$ is small for large $m,n$. In $\mathbb R^m$ (with either metric above), a sequence converges exactly when each coordinate converges.

A set $F$ is **closed** if it contains the limit of every convergent sequence of points of $F$. $[0,1]$ is closed; $(0,1]$ is not, because $1/n\to0\notin(0,1]$.

$X$ is **complete** if every Cauchy sequence converges to a point of $X$. $\mathbb R$ and $\mathbb R^m$ are complete; $\mathbb Q$ is not; neither is $(0,1]$ (the sequence $1/n$ is Cauchy with no limit in the set). The continuous functions on $[a,b]$ with $d_\infty$ are complete: this follows from the Cauchy criterion for uniform convergence and Theorem 8.1 (Abbott Section 6.2). Completeness is what Module 0.7 needs to prove that value iteration converges.

### 6.4 Compactness

**Definition.** $K\subseteq X$ is **compact** if every sequence in $K$ has a subsequence that converges to a point of $K$. (In metric spaces this is equivalent to the open-cover definition in Rudin chapter 2.)

In plain words: a sequence in a compact set cannot escape, either off to infinity or toward a missing boundary point.

**Heine–Borel.** In $\mathbb R^m$, a set is compact if and only if it is closed and bounded.

*Why "closed and bounded" suffices:* a bounded sequence in $\mathbb R^m$ has bounded coordinates. Apply Bolzano–Weierstrass to the first coordinate to get a subsequence where it converges; then to the second coordinate along that subsequence; and so on through all $m$ coordinates. The final subsequence converges in every coordinate, hence in $\mathbb R^m$, and closedness keeps the limit inside $K$.

Non-examples: $(0,1]$ is bounded but not closed (the sequence $1/n$ escapes to $0$); $[0,\infty)$ is closed but not bounded (the sequence $n$ escapes to infinity).

**Theorem 6.1.** If $K$ is compact and $f:K\to\mathbb R$ is continuous, then $f$ attains its maximum and minimum on $K$, and $f$ is uniformly continuous on $K$. The proofs of Theorems 5.1 and 5.2 work word for word, with "compact" in place of Bolzano–Weierstrass on $[a,b]$.

This is the existence engine of optimization: **a continuous cost over a compact set of decisions has an optimal decision.** The probability simplex $\Delta_m=\{p\in\mathbb R^m:p_i\ge0,\ \sum_ip_i=1\}$ is compact, so an optimal randomized rule exists (Problem C1). When an optimizer seems to run off without converging, one of these hypotheses is usually failing: the decision set is unbounded, or open at the edge where the optimum would be.

### 6.5 Infinite dimensions are different

In the space of summable sequences with the $\ell_1$ distance, let $e_n$ have a $1$ in place $n$ and $0$ elsewhere. Then $\|e_n-e_m\|_1=2$ for $n\ne m$, so no subsequence is Cauchy, let alone convergent. The set $\{e_n\}$ is closed and bounded but not compact: Heine–Borel fails. In probability terms, $e_n$ is the point mass on state $n$, and a sequence of distributions can "escape to infinity" through ever larger states. That is why optimal policies for MDPs with infinitely many states need extra conditions (Module 4.4), and why Module 1.4 introduces *tightness*.

The same happens for functions. In $C[0,1]$ with $d_\infty$, the functions $x^n$ all lie within distance $1$ of $0$, but no subsequence converges uniformly: any subsequence converges pointwise to the discontinuous limit ($0$ on $[0,1)$, $1$ at $1$), and a uniform limit of continuous functions would have to be continuous (Theorem 8.1).

**Quick checks.**

1. Is the closed unit disc $\{(x,y):x^2+y^2\le1\}$ compact?
2. Is $\{(p_1,p_2):p_1,p_2>0,\ p_1+p_2=1\}$ compact?
3. What is the total variation distance between point masses on two different states?

*Answers.* (1) Yes: closed and bounded. (2) No: it is not closed, since $(\frac1n,1-\frac1n)\to(0,1)$, which is outside the set. (3) $1$ (their $\ell_1$ distance is $2$).

## 7. The Riemann integral and where it breaks

### 7.1 Area from above and below

The idea: trap the area under a graph between rectangles that lie under it and rectangles that lie over it, and refine until the gap disappears (picture 4).

Take a bounded $f$ on $[a,b]$ and a partition $P:\ a=x_0<x_1<\dots<x_k=b$. With $M_i=\sup f$ and $m_i=\inf f$ on the piece $[x_{i-1},x_i]$ and $\Delta x_i=x_i-x_{i-1}$, set
$$U(f,P)=\sum_iM_i\Delta x_i\ \ (\text{upper sum}),\qquad L(f,P)=\sum_im_i\Delta x_i\ \ (\text{lower sum}).$$

*Worked example.* $f(x)=x$ on $[0,1]$ with $n$ equal pieces. On the piece $[\frac{i-1}n,\frac in]$ the sup is $\frac in$ and the inf is $\frac{i-1}n$, so
$$U=\sum_{i=1}^n\frac in\cdot\frac1n=\frac{n+1}{2n},\qquad L=\sum_{i=1}^n\frac{i-1}n\cdot\frac1n=\frac{n-1}{2n},\qquad U-L=\frac1n .$$
Both tend to $\frac12$, the area of the triangle.

### 7.2 The definition and the test

Adding points to a partition can only lower each sup on the smaller pieces and raise each inf, so **refining lowers $U$ and raises $L$**. Hence for any two partitions $P,P'$, using their common refinement $P\cup P'$,
$$L(f,P)\le L(f,P\cup P')\le U(f,P\cup P')\le U(f,P'):$$
every lower sum is below every upper sum. $f$ is **(Riemann) integrable** if $\inf_PU(f,P)=\sup_PL(f,P)$, and the common value is $\int_a^bf$.

**Criterion.** $f$ is integrable if and only if for every $\varepsilon>0$ there is a partition with $U(f,P)-L(f,P)<\varepsilon$ (Abbott Section 7.2). The reason: $\sup L\le\inf U$ always, and a partition with $U-L<\varepsilon$ squeezes the gap between them below $\varepsilon$.

### 7.3 Which functions are integrable

**Theorem 7.1.** A continuous function on $[a,b]$ is integrable.

*Plan.* Uniform continuity makes the sup and inf on every short piece close together.

*Proof.* By Theorem 5.2 choose $\delta$ with $|x-y|<\delta\Rightarrow|f(x)-f(y)|<\varepsilon/(b-a)$, and a partition with every $\Delta x_i<\delta$. On each piece, $M_i$ and $m_i$ are attained (Theorem 5.1) at two points less than $\delta$ apart, so $M_i-m_i<\varepsilon/(b-a)$. Hence $U-L=\sum_i(M_i-m_i)\Delta x_i<\varepsilon$. $\blacksquare$

**Monotone functions are integrable too**, even with jumps. For increasing $f$ and $n$ equal pieces, $M_i=f(x_i)$ and $m_i=f(x_{i-1})$, so the sum telescopes: $U-L=\big(f(b)-f(a)\big)\frac{b-a}n\to0$. Distribution functions are increasing, so they can always be integrated this way.

### 7.4 Properties and the fundamental theorem

The integral is linear, monotone ($f\le g\Rightarrow\int f\le\int g$), additive over adjacent intervals, and satisfies $\big|\int f\big|\le\int|f|$ (Abbott Section 7.4).

**Fundamental theorem of calculus** (Abbott Section 7.5).

- (i) If $F$ is differentiable on $[a,b]$ and $F'=f$ is integrable, then $\int_a^bf=F(b)-F(a)$.
- (ii) If $f$ is integrable, then $G(x)=\int_a^xf$ is continuous, and $G'(c)=f(c)$ at every point $c$ where $f$ is continuous.

The intuition for (ii): $G(c+h)-G(c)$ is the area of a thin strip of width $h$ and height about $f(c)$.

*At the stand.* If the time to the next taxi has density $\lambda e^{-\lambda t}$, the probability that it arrives by time $T$ is $\int_0^T\lambda e^{-\lambda t}dt=\big[-e^{-\lambda t}\big]_0^T=1-e^{-\lambda T}$, by (i). With $\lambda=2$ per minute and $T=1.5$ minutes, that is $0.950$.

### 7.5 Where Riemann breaks

**Pointwise limits.** List the rationals of $[0,1]$ as $q_1,q_2,\dots$ (they are countable, Module 0.1) and let $g_n$ be the indicator of $\{q_1,\dots,q_n\}$. Each $g_n$ is integrable with $\int g_n=0$, since it is $0$ except at finitely many points. The $g_n$ increase to the Dirichlet function $g=\mathbf 1_{\mathbb Q\cap[0,1]}$. But $g$ is not Riemann integrable: every piece of every partition contains rationals and irrationals, so $U(g,P)=1$ and $L(g,P)=0$. An increasing limit of functions with integral $0$ has no integral at all.

**Unbounded functions and infinite intervals.** The Riemann integral is defined only for bounded functions on bounded intervals. $\int_0^\infty\lambda e^{-\lambda t}dt$ and $\int_0^1x^{-1/2}dx$ have to be patched as limits ("improper integrals"), case by case.

Probability needs all three at once: limits of random variables, infinite time horizons, and unbounded densities. Module 0.5 replaces the Riemann integral with the Lebesgue integral, which handles them uniformly. Under it, $\int g=0$ for the Dirichlet function, and increasing limits behave.

**Quick checks.**

1. For $f(x)=2x$ on $[0,1]$ with $n$ equal pieces, what is $U-L$?
2. Is $\mathbf 1_{[0,1/2]}$ integrable on $[0,1]$? What is its integral?
3. What is $\frac{d}{dx}\int_0^xe^{-t^2}dt$?

*Answers.* (1) $2/n$, by the monotone formula $(f(1)-f(0))\cdot\frac1n$. (2) Yes, since it is monotone (decreasing); the integral is $\frac12$. (3) $e^{-x^2}$, by part (ii), since $e^{-t^2}$ is continuous.

## 8. Uniform convergence: when limits can be swapped

### 8.1 Pointwise versus uniform

Let $f_n$ and $f$ be functions on a set $A$.

- $f_n\to f$ **pointwise** if $f_n(x)\to f(x)$ for each $x\in A$. In the game, the opponent picks $x$ and $\varepsilon$, then you pick $N$: **$N$ may depend on $x$.**
- $f_n\to f$ **uniformly** if $\sup_{x\in A}|f_n(x)-f(x)|\to0$. Now you must pick $N$ knowing only $\varepsilon$: **one $N$ works for every $x$.** Equivalently, $d_\infty(f_n,f)\to0$: from some $n$ on, the whole graph of $f_n$ lies in the band of half-width $\varepsilon$ around the graph of $f$.

*Worked example.* $f_n(x)=x^n$ on $[0,1]$. For fixed $x<1$, $x^n\to0$, and $x^n<\varepsilon$ once $n>\ln\varepsilon/\ln x$. As $x\to1$, $\ln x\to0$ and this $N$ blows up: the closer $x$ is to $1$, the longer you wait. That is pointwise convergence with no uniform $N$, and indeed $\sup_{x<1}x^n=1$ for every $n$. On $[0,a]$ with $a<1$, the sup is $a^n\to0$: uniform.

Other examples: $x+\frac1n\to x$ uniformly on $\mathbb R$ (the gap is exactly $\frac1n$ everywhere). $\frac{\sin(nx)}n\to0$ uniformly, with sup $\frac1n$. $\frac xn\to0$ pointwise on $\mathbb R$ but not uniformly, since $\sup_{x\in\mathbb R}|x/n|=\infty$; on $[0,1]$ it is uniform.

### 8.2 Continuity survives uniform limits

**Theorem 8.1.** A uniform limit of continuous functions is continuous (Problem B6). The proof is the "$\varepsilon/3$ argument": to compare $f(x)$ with $f(c)$, go through $f_n(x)$ and $f_n(c)$ for one good $n$.

The example $x^n$ shows that a pointwise limit can break continuity. Turned around, Theorem 8.1 is a quick test: if continuous functions converge to a discontinuous limit, the convergence was not uniform.

### 8.3 Integrals survive uniform limits on bounded intervals

**Theorem 8.2 (integrals).** If each $f_n$ is integrable on $[a,b]$ and $f_n\to f$ uniformly, then $f$ is integrable and $\int_a^bf_n\to\int_a^bf$.

*Plan.* A uniformly small gap $\delta_n$ between $f_n$ and $f$ changes every upper and lower sum, and the integral, by at most $\delta_n(b-a)$.

*Proof.* Let $\delta_n=\sup_x|f_n(x)-f(x)|\to0$; $f$ is bounded since $|f|\le|f_n|+\delta_n$. Given $\varepsilon>0$, pick $n$ with $\delta_n(b-a)<\varepsilon/3$ and a partition $P$ with $U(f_n,P)-L(f_n,P)<\varepsilon/3$. On every piece $f_n-\delta_n\le f\le f_n+\delta_n$, so $U(f,P)\le U(f_n,P)+\delta_n(b-a)$ and $L(f,P)\ge L(f_n,P)-\delta_n(b-a)$, which gives $U(f,P)-L(f,P)<\varepsilon$. So $f$ is integrable, and integrating $f_n-\delta_n\le f\le f_n+\delta_n$ gives $\big|\int f_n-\int f\big|\le\delta_n(b-a)\to0$. $\blacksquare$

**Without uniformity it fails.** The tent $h_n$, defined by $h_n(t)=n^2t$ on $[0,1/n]$, $h_n(t)=n^2(2/n-t)$ on $[1/n,2/n]$ and $0$ elsewhere on $[0,2]$, has height $n$ and base $[0,2/n]$, so $\int h_n=1$. But $h_n\to0$ at every point (Problem I3). The mass escapes into an ever thinner spike. Note also the factor $b-a$ in the proof: on an infinite interval, a uniformly small gap can still add up to a large integral, as $\frac1n\mathbf 1_{[0,n]}$ shows (it converges uniformly to $0$, but its integral is $1$ for every $n$).

### 8.4 Derivatives need more

**Theorem 8.3 (derivatives).** Suppose each $f_n$ has a continuous derivative on $[a,b]$, $f_n'\to g$ uniformly, and $f_n(a)\to A$. Then $f_n\to f$ uniformly, where $f(x)=A+\int_a^xg$, and $f'=g$.

*Plan.* Write each $f_n$ as the integral of its derivative, and pass to the limit inside the integral with Theorem 8.2.

*Proof.* By the fundamental theorem (i), $f_n(x)=f_n(a)+\int_a^xf_n'$. The limit $g$ is continuous (Theorem 8.1), so $f$ is well defined and $f'=g$ by the fundamental theorem (ii). Finally $|f_n(x)-f(x)|\le|f_n(a)-A|+(b-a)\sup|f_n'-g|\to0$, uniformly in $x$. $\blacksquare$

The hypothesis is on the *derivatives*, and it cannot be dropped. The functions $f_n(x)=\sin(nx)/\sqrt n$ converge to $0$ uniformly, but $f_n'(x)=\sqrt n\cos(nx)$ converges at no point at all (at $x=0$, for example, it equals $\sqrt n$). Uniformly close functions can have wildly different slopes. Remember this when you differentiate a smooth surrogate of a simulated cost: a surrogate that fits the cost well can still have the wrong gradient.

### 8.5 Series of functions

Apply Theorems 8.1–8.3 to partial sums $S_N=\sum_{n\le N}f_n$.

**Weierstrass M-test.** If $|f_n(x)|\le M_n$ for all $x\in A$ and $\sum M_n<\infty$, then $\sum f_n$ converges uniformly and absolutely on $A$ (Problem B7). The bound $M_n$ must not depend on $x$; that is what makes the convergence uniform.

*Uniformization.* For $0\le t\le T$, the weights satisfy $0\le e^{-\Lambda t}(\Lambda t)^n/n!\le(\Lambda T)^n/n!$, which is summable. So the series for $p(t)$ converges uniformly on $[0,T]$, and $p$ is continuous there. Problem T1 gives the sharper bound you will actually use, and Problem I1 explains why the convergence is not uniform on $[0,\infty)$.

**Power series.** A power series $\sum c_nx^n$ has a *radius of convergence* $R\in[0,\infty]$: it converges absolutely for $|x|<R$ and diverges for $|x|>R$. On $[-r,r]$ for every $r<R$ it converges uniformly (M-test with $M_n=|c_n|r^n$), and it can be differentiated and integrated term by term there (Abbott Section 6.5).

*Worked example: the mean queue length.* $\sum_{k\ge0}x^k=\frac1{1-x}$ for $|x|<1$ (radius $1$). Differentiating term by term, $\sum_{k\ge1}kx^{k-1}=\frac1{(1-x)^2}$, so $\sum_kk\rho^k=\frac{\rho}{(1-\rho)^2}$. For the queue of Section 4.2 with $\pi_k=(1-\rho)\rho^k$, the mean number of waiting passengers is
$$\sum_kk\,\pi_k=(1-\rho)\frac{\rho}{(1-\rho)^2}=\frac{\rho}{1-\rho}.$$
At $\rho=0.8$ that is $4$ passengers on average; at $\rho=0.95$ it is $19$. The convergence is uniform on $[-r,r]$ for each $r<1$ but not on $(-1,1)$, and the blow-up as $\rho\to1$ is the familiar explosion of queues near full utilization. Generating functions in Module 1.3 produce moments by exactly this differentiation.

### 8.6 Differentiating under the integral sign

**Theorem 8.4.** Let $f(x,\theta)$ and $\partial f/\partial\theta$ be continuous on the rectangle $[a,b]\times[c,d]$. Then $J(\theta)=\int_a^bf(x,\theta)\,dx$ is differentiable on $[c,d]$, and
$$J'(\theta)=\int_a^b\frac{\partial f}{\partial\theta}(x,\theta)\,dx .$$

*Plan.* By the mean value theorem, each difference quotient in $\theta$ equals $\partial_\theta f$ at a nearby point; uniform continuity on the compact rectangle makes "nearby" uniform in $x$.

*Proof.* Fix $\theta$ and $\varepsilon>0$. The rectangle is compact (Heine–Borel), so $\partial_\theta f$ is uniformly continuous on it (Theorem 6.1): there is $\delta>0$ with $|\partial_\theta f(x,\theta')-\partial_\theta f(x,\theta)|<\varepsilon$ whenever $|\theta'-\theta|<\delta$, for every $x$. Take $0<|h|<\delta$ with $\theta+h\in[c,d]$. For each $x$, the mean value theorem in $\theta$ gives some $\theta'$ between $\theta$ and $\theta+h$ with $\big(f(x,\theta+h)-f(x,\theta)\big)/h=\partial_\theta f(x,\theta')$. So the continuous function
$$D_h(x)=\frac{f(x,\theta+h)-f(x,\theta)}{h}-\partial_\theta f(x,\theta)$$
satisfies $|D_h(x)|<\varepsilon$ for every $x$. Integrating, $\Big|\frac{J(\theta+h)-J(\theta)}{h}-\int_a^b\partial_\theta f(x,\theta)\,dx\Big|=\Big|\int_a^bD_h\Big|\le\varepsilon(b-a)$. $\blacksquare$

*Example.* $\frac{d}{d\theta}\int_0^1e^{\theta x}dx=\int_0^1xe^{\theta x}dx$; both integrands are continuous on $[0,1]\times[c,d]$ for any $c<d$. Problem C2 does a full taxi-stand example and checks the answer against a closed form.

This is the theorem behind pathwise (IPA-type) gradient estimators in Module 3.6: the derivative of an expected cost equals the expected derivative when this kind of uniform control holds. When it fails, as with the moving tent, swapping is wrong and the gradient estimate is biased.

### 8.7 Swapping sums, and limits inside sums

**Double sums.** If every $a_{mn}\ge0$, then $\sum_m\sum_na_{mn}=\sum_n\sum_ma_{mn}$, both sides possibly $+\infty$. The same holds when $\sum_m\sum_n|a_{mn}|<\infty$ (Abbott Section 2.8). Without either condition the order matters. Let $a_{mm}=1$, $a_{m,m+1}=-1$ and all other $a_{mn}=0$:
$$\begin{array}{c|ccccc}&n=1&n=2&n=3&n=4&\cdots\\\hline m=1&1&-1&0&0&\\m=2&0&1&-1&0&\\m=3&0&0&1&-1&\\\vdots&&&&&\ddots\end{array}$$
Every row sums to $0$, so $\sum_m\sum_na_{mn}=0$. Column $1$ sums to $1$ and every other column sums to $1-1=0$, so $\sum_n\sum_ma_{mn}=1$. In probability, $E\big[\sum_nX_n\big]=\sum_nE[X_n]$ for nonnegative $X_n$ is the same statement; Module 0.5 proves the integral version.

**Limits inside sums.** If $a_{k,n}\to a_n$ as $k\to\infty$ for each $n$, and $|a_{k,n}|\le b_n$ with $\sum_nb_n<\infty$, then $\sum_na_{k,n}\to\sum_na_n$ (Problem B8). A summable bound that does not depend on $k$ is what licenses the swap. This is the series form of the **dominated convergence theorem** (Module 0.5), the theorem you will use most often in this course. Without the bound, mass can run off to infinity: $a_{k,n}=1$ if $n=k$ and $0$ otherwise has $\sum_na_{k,n}=1$ for every $k$ but $a_{k,n}\to0$ for each $n$.

### 8.8 Summary: what licenses each swap

| To swap | It is enough that |
|---|---|
| a limit and continuity | the convergence is uniform (Theorem 8.1) |
| a limit and $\int_a^b$ on a bounded interval | the convergence is uniform (Theorem 8.2), or there is a dominating bound (Module 0.5) |
| a limit and $\frac{d}{dx}$ | the *derivatives* converge uniformly (Theorem 8.3) |
| $\frac{d}{d\theta}$ and $\int_a^b$ | $f$ and $\partial_\theta f$ are continuous on a compact rectangle (Theorem 8.4) |
| two infinite sums | the terms are nonnegative, or absolutely summable |
| a limit and an infinite sum | a summable bound independent of $k$ (Tannery, Problem B8) |

### I2

#### Solution

### B5

#### Solution
*Absolute convergence:* each partial sum $|b_1|+\dots+|b_m|$ is a sum of distinct terms $|a_n|$, so it is at most $\sum_n|a_n|$. The partial sums increase and are bounded, so they converge.

*The sum is $S$:* let $\varepsilon>0$. Choose $N$ with $\sum_{n>N}|a_n|<\varepsilon/2$, and write $s_N=a_1+\dots+a_N$. Then $|S-s_N|=\big|\sum_{n>N}a_n\big|\le\sum_{n>N}|a_n|<\varepsilon/2$. Let $M=\max\{\sigma^{-1}(1),\dots,\sigma^{-1}(N)\}$. For $m\ge M$, the partial sum $t_m=b_1+\dots+b_m$ contains $a_1,\dots,a_N$ plus finitely many distinct $a_n$ with $n>N$. So $|t_m-s_N|\le\sum_{n>N}|a_n|<\varepsilon/2$, and $|t_m-S|<\varepsilon$. Hence $t_m\to S$.

### B6
(a) Prove that if each $f_n$ is continuous on $A$ and $f_n\to f$ uniformly on $A$, then $f$ is continuous. (b) Deduce, without computing any sup, that $x^n$ does not converge uniformly on $[0,1]$. (c) Show that the converse of (a) is false: continuous $f_n$ can converge pointwise to a continuous $f$ without converging uniformly.

#### Hint
(a) is the "$\varepsilon/3$ argument": go from $f(x)$ to $f_n(x)$, then to $f_n(c)$, then to $f(c)$.

#### Solution
(a) Fix $c\in A$ and $\varepsilon>0$. Choose $n$ with $\sup_A|f_n-f|<\varepsilon/3$. Since $f_n$ is continuous at $c$, choose $\delta>0$ with $|f_n(x)-f_n(c)|<\varepsilon/3$ whenever $x\in A$, $|x-c|<\delta$. For such $x$,
$$|f(x)-f(c)|\le|f(x)-f_n(x)|+|f_n(x)-f_n(c)|+|f_n(c)-f(c)|<\varepsilon .$$

(b) Each $x^n$ is continuous, but the pointwise limit (0 on $[0,1)$, 1 at $1$) is not continuous at $1$. If the convergence were uniform, (a) would make the limit continuous.

(c) The tents $h_n$ of Section 8 are continuous and converge pointwise to the continuous function $0$, but $\sup h_n=n$, so the convergence is not uniform. (So is $nxe^{-nx}$ on $[0,\infty)$ from A5(c), whose sup stays at $e^{-1}$.)

### B7
(a) Prove the Weierstrass M-test: if $|f_n(x)|\le M_n$ for all $x\in A$ and $\sum M_n<\infty$, then $\sum f_n$ converges uniformly on $A$. (b) Deduce that $\sum_{n\ge0}x^n/n!$ converges uniformly on $[-R,R]$ for every $R$, and that $e^x$ is continuous on $\mathbb R$. (c) Show that the convergence is not uniform on all of $\mathbb R$.

#### Hint
(a) Use the Cauchy criterion for $\sum M_n$ to make $|S_N(x)-S_M(x)|$ small for all $x$ at once.

#### Solution
(a) Let $S_N(x)=\sum_{n\le N}f_n(x)$. For $N>M$ and every $x\in A$, $|S_N(x)-S_M(x)|\le\sum_{n=M+1}^NM_n\le\sum_{n>M}M_n$, and the right side tends to $0$ as $M\to\infty$, independently of $x$. So for each $x$ the sequence $S_N(x)$ is Cauchy and converges to some $S(x)$. Letting $N\to\infty$ gives $|S(x)-S_M(x)|\le\sum_{n>M}M_n$ for every $x$, so $\sup_A|S-S_M|\to0$.

(b) On $[-R,R]$, $|x^n/n!|\le R^n/n!$, and $\sum R^n/n!$ converges by the ratio test (ratio $R/(n+1)\to0$). So the series converges uniformly on $[-R,R]$. Its partial sums are polynomials, hence continuous, so the limit $e^x$ is continuous on $[-R,R]$ by Problem B6. Every real $x$ lies inside some $(-R,R)$, so $e^x$ is continuous at every point of $\mathbb R$.

(c) Fix $M$. For $x>0$ all terms are positive, so $e^x-S_M(x)\ge x^{M+1}/(M+1)!\to\infty$ as $x\to\infty$. Hence $\sup_{\mathbb R}|e^x-S_M(x)|=\infty$ for every $M$, and the convergence is not uniform on $\mathbb R$. The same thing happens to uniformization in time: uniform on $[0,T]$, not on $[0,\infty)$ (Problem I1).

### B8
(a) Prove **Tannery's theorem**: if $a_{k,n}\to a_n$ as $k\to\infty$ for each $n$, and $|a_{k,n}|\le b_n$ for all $k,n$ with $\sum_nb_n<\infty$, then $\sum_na_{k,n}\to\sum_na_n$. (b) Use it to prove $(1+x/k)^k\to\sum_{n\ge0}x^n/n!=e^x$ for every real $x$. What does $x=-\lambda$ say about passenger arrivals? (c) Show that the bound $b_n$ cannot be dropped, using $a_{k,n}=1$ if $n=k$ and $0$ otherwise.

#### Hint
(a) Split the sum at a fixed $N$: the first $N$ terms converge one by one; the tails are small uniformly in $k$ because of $b_n$. (b) Expand by the binomial theorem and write $\binom kn\frac1{k^n}=\frac1{n!}\prod_{j=0}^{n-1}\big(1-\frac jk\big)$.

#### Solution
(a) Since $|a_n|\le b_n$, $\sum a_n$ converges absolutely, and so does each $\sum_na_{k,n}$. Given $\varepsilon>0$, choose $N$ with $\sum_{n>N}b_n<\varepsilon/3$. Then
$$\Big|\sum_na_{k,n}-\sum_na_n\Big|\le\sum_{n\le N}|a_{k,n}-a_n|+\sum_{n>N}\big(|a_{k,n}|+|a_n|\big)\le\sum_{n\le N}|a_{k,n}-a_n|+\frac{2\varepsilon}3 .$$
The first sum has finitely many terms, each tending to $0$, so it is below $\varepsilon/3$ for all large $k$.

(b) By the binomial theorem, $(1+x/k)^k=\sum_{n\ge0}a_{k,n}$ with $a_{k,n}=\frac{x^n}{n!}\prod_{j=0}^{n-1}\big(1-\frac jk\big)$ for $n\le k$ and $a_{k,n}=0$ for $n>k$. For $n\le k$ each factor lies in $(0,1]$, so $|a_{k,n}|\le|x|^n/n!=:b_n$, which is summable. For fixed $n$, the product tends to $1$ as $k\to\infty$, so $a_{k,n}\to x^n/n!$. Tannery's theorem gives $(1+x/k)^k\to\sum_nx^n/n!=e^x$.

With $x=-\lambda$: split an hour into $k$ slots and let a passenger arrive in each slot independently with probability $\lambda/k$. The chance that nobody arrives is $(1-\lambda/k)^k\to e^{-\lambda}$, the Poisson probability of zero arrivals. Module 1.4 turns this into the Poisson limit theorem.

(c) Here $\sum_na_{k,n}=1$ for every $k$, but $a_{k,n}\to0$ for each fixed $n$, so $\sum_n\lim_ka_{k,n}=0\ne1$. No summable bound exists, since any $b_n\ge\sup_ka_{k,n}=1$ for every $n$. The unit of mass runs off to infinity: the discrete version of the moving tent.

### C1
A dispatcher picks one of $m$ release rules at random, rule $i$ with probability $p_i$, so a randomized rule is a point $p$ of the simplex $\Delta_m$. Its expected cost $c(p)$ is any continuous function, for example $c(p)=\sum_ic_ip_i+\gamma\,p^{\mathsf T}Ap$. (a) Prove that $\Delta_m$ is compact. (b) Conclude that an optimal randomized rule exists. (c) Show that both halves of "closed and bounded" matter: give a continuous cost of a release rate $\theta$ with no minimizer on $[0,\infty)$, and one with no minimizer on $(0,1]$.

#### Hint
(a) Heine–Borel: show bounded, then closed using limits coordinate by coordinate.

#### Solution
(a) *Bounded:* $0\le p_i\le1$ for every $i$. *Closed:* let $p^{(k)}\in\Delta_m$ with $p^{(k)}\to p$ in $\mathbb R^m$. Then each coordinate converges, so $p_i=\lim_kp^{(k)}_i\ge0$ by the order limit theorem, and $\sum_ip_i=\lim_k\sum_ip^{(k)}_i=1$, since a finite sum of limits is the limit of the sums. So $p\in\Delta_m$. By Heine–Borel, $\Delta_m$ is compact.

(b) By Theorem 6.1, the continuous function $c$ attains its minimum on the compact set $\Delta_m$: some $p^*$ has $c(p^*)\le c(p)$ for every randomized rule $p$. (The example cost is a polynomial in $p$, hence continuous.)

(c) *Unbounded:* $f(\theta)=1/(1+\theta)$ on $[0,\infty)$, a waiting penalty that keeps falling as taxis are released faster. Its inf is $0$, never attained. *Not closed:* $f(\theta)=\theta$ on $(0,1]$. Its inf is $0$, approached as $\theta\to0$, but $0$ is not an allowed rate. In practice, missing optima usually mean the decision set needs a bound or should include its boundary.

### C2
Passengers arrive uniformly over an hour, $x\in[0,1]$. With release rate $\theta\in[\theta_{\min},\theta_{\max}]$, $0<\theta_{\min}$, a passenger who arrives at $x$ is still waiting at the end of the hour with probability $e^{-\theta(1-x)}$. So the expected fraction still waiting is $J(\theta)=\int_0^1e^{-\theta(1-x)}\,dx$. (a) Justify differentiating under the integral and write $J'(\theta)$ as an integral. (b) Compute $J$ and $J'$ in closed form, and check that $J'$ really is the derivative of $J$. (c) Give $J(2)$ and $J'(2)$. (d) In one sentence: what does autograd in your code compute, and what does Theorem 8.4 add?

#### Hint
(a) Check the hypotheses of Theorem 8.4. (b) Substitute $u=1-x$ and integrate $ue^{-\theta u}$ by parts.

#### Solution
(a) $f(x,\theta)=e^{-\theta(1-x)}$ and $\partial_\theta f=-(1-x)e^{-\theta(1-x)}$ are continuous on $[0,1]\times[\theta_{\min},\theta_{\max}]$. By Theorem 8.4,
$$J'(\theta)=-\int_0^1(1-x)e^{-\theta(1-x)}\,dx .$$

(b) With $u=1-x$: $J(\theta)=\int_0^1e^{-\theta u}du=\dfrac{1-e^{-\theta}}{\theta}$. By parts, $\int_0^1ue^{-\theta u}du=\Big[-\frac{u}{\theta}e^{-\theta u}\Big]_0^1+\frac1\theta\int_0^1e^{-\theta u}du=\dfrac{1-e^{-\theta}-\theta e^{-\theta}}{\theta^2}$. So
$$J'(\theta)=-\frac{1-(1+\theta)e^{-\theta}}{\theta^2}.$$
Check by differentiating $J$ with the quotient rule: $\dfrac{\theta e^{-\theta}-(1-e^{-\theta})}{\theta^2}=\dfrac{(1+\theta)e^{-\theta}-1}{\theta^2}$, the same.

(c) $J(2)=\frac{1-e^{-2}}2=0.43233$ and $J'(2)=-\frac{1-3e^{-2}}4=-0.14850$. (A central difference with step $10^{-6}$ gives the same to seven digits.) A faster release rate lowers the fraction left waiting, as it should.

(d) Autograd differentiates the computation you wrote, for example a quadrature sum or a truncated series. Theorem 8.4 (with Theorems 8.2–8.3 for limits of such sums) is what guarantees that this derivative converges to the derivative of the true expected cost.

### C3
Drivers decide whether to come to the airport. If a fraction $x$ of the fleet joins, the queue is longer and fewer want to join. A logit choice model gives the equilibrium as a fixed point $x=g(x)$ with $g(x)=\dfrac{1}{1+e^{2x}}$. (a) Show that $g$ maps $[0,1]$ into $[0,1]$ and that $|g'(x)|\le\frac12$. (b) With $x_0=0$ and $x_{k+1}=g(x_k)$, prove that $(x_k)$ converges to a fixed point $x^*$ and that the fixed point is unique. (c) Prove $|x_k-x^*|\le2^{-k}$ and find $k$ that guarantees error at most $10^{-6}$. Compare with the actual error.

#### Hint
$g'=-2g(1-g)$, and $u(1-u)\le\frac14$. Then use the mean value theorem: $|g(a)-g(b)|\le\frac12|a-b|$.

#### Solution
(a) $0<g(x)<1$ for every real $x$. Differentiating, $g'(x)=-\dfrac{2e^{2x}}{(1+e^{2x})^2}=-2g(x)\big(1-g(x)\big)$, and $u(1-u)\le\frac14$ for all $u$, so $|g'|\le\frac12$.

(b) By the mean value theorem, $|g(a)-g(b)|\le\frac12|a-b|$. So $|x_{k+1}-x_k|=|g(x_k)-g(x_{k-1})|\le\frac12|x_k-x_{k-1}|$, and by induction $|x_{k+1}-x_k|\le2^{-k}|x_1-x_0|$. For $m>k$,
$$|x_m-x_k|\le\sum_{j=k}^{m-1}2^{-j}|x_1-x_0|<2^{1-k}|x_1-x_0|\to0,$$
so $(x_k)$ is Cauchy and converges to some $x^*$ (Theorem 2.3). Since $g$ is continuous, $x^*=\lim x_{k+1}=\lim g(x_k)=g(x^*)$. If also $g(y)=y$, then $|x^*-y|=|g(x^*)-g(y)|\le\frac12|x^*-y|$, which forces $y=x^*$.

(c) Let $m\to\infty$ in the display: $|x^*-x_k|\le2^{1-k}|x_1-x_0|=2^{1-k}\cdot\frac12=2^{-k}$, since $x_1=g(0)=\frac12$. This is at most $10^{-6}$ once $2^k\ge10^6$, that is $k\ge20$ ($2^{20}=1{,}048{,}576$).

Numerically $x^*=0.3374158$, and the actual error at $k=20$ is $3.5\times10^{-8}$, about 27 times smaller than the guarantee. The reason is that near $x^*$ the slope is $|g'(x^*)|=2x^*(1-x^*)=0.447$, not $\frac12$. The bound is a guarantee, not an estimate. The same argument, in a space of value functions, proves value iteration converges (Modules 0.7 and 4.2).

### D1
In Python: (a) For $f_n(x)=x^n$, compute $\sup|f_n-f|$ on a grid of $[0,1]$ and on $[0,0.9]$ for $n=10,100,1000,100{,}000$. What does the grid get wrong? (b) Build the taxi stand as a birth–death chain on $z\in\{-5,\dots,40\}$ ($z<0$: $-z$ taxis waiting; $z>0$: $z$ passengers waiting), with taxi arrivals at rate $1$ per minute and passenger arrivals at rate $1.2$ per minute, starting empty. Compute $p(t)$ on $[0,60]$ by uniformization with $N$ chosen from Lemma 4.1 for tolerances $10^{-6}$ and $10^{-10}$, compare with `scipy.linalg.expm`, and check that the error stays below the bound at every time. (c) Keep $N$ fixed and look at $t=120$ and $240$.

#### Hint
Precompute $v_n=p(0)P^n$ once; then $p_N(t)=\sum_{n\le N}w_n(t)v_n$ is one matrix product for all times. Use `scipy.stats.poisson.pmf` for the weights $w_n(t)$.

#### Solution
```python
import math
import numpy as np
from scipy.linalg import expm
from scipy.stats import poisson

# (a) pointwise but not uniform: f_n(x) = x^n on [0,1]
x = np.linspace(0, 1, 10_001)
f = (x == 1.0).astype(float)          # pointwise limit
for n in (10, 100, 1000, 100_000):
    on_01 = np.abs(x**n - f).max()
    on_09 = np.abs(x[x <= 0.9]**n).max()
    print(f"n={n:>6}: grid sup on [0,1] = {on_01:.2e}   sup on [0,0.9] = {on_09:.2e}")

# (b) uniformization for the taxi stand, checked against expm
K, M = 5, 40                  # at most K taxis wait; at most M passengers wait
alpha, beta = 1.0, 1.2        # taxi and passenger arrival rates per minute
states = np.arange(-K, M + 1) # z < 0: -z taxis waiting; z > 0: z passengers waiting
S = len(states)
Q = np.zeros((S, S))
for i, z in enumerate(states):
    if z > -K:
        Q[i, i - 1] = alpha   # a taxi arrives (and is matched if passengers wait)
    if z < M:
        Q[i, i + 1] = beta    # a passenger arrives
    Q[i, i] = -Q[i].sum()
Lam = alpha + beta            # largest total exit rate
P = np.eye(S) + Q / Lam
assert (P >= 0).all() and np.allclose(P.sum(axis=1), 1)
p0 = np.zeros(S); p0[K] = 1.0 # start empty: z = 0
T = 60.0

def tail_bound(lam, N):       # Lemma 4.1; needs N + 2 > lam
    return math.exp(-lam + (N + 1) * math.log(lam) - math.lgamma(N + 2)) / (1 - lam / (N + 2))

def choose_N(tol, lam):
    N = math.ceil(lam)
    while tail_bound(lam, N) > tol:
        N += 1
    return N

ts = np.linspace(0, T, 241)
exact = np.array([p0 @ expm(Q * t) for t in ts])
for tol in (1e-6, 1e-10):
    N = choose_N(tol, Lam * T)
    V = np.zeros((N + 1, S)); V[0] = p0
    for n in range(N):
        V[n + 1] = V[n] @ P               # V[n] = p0 P^n
    W = poisson.pmf(np.arange(N + 1)[None, :], Lam * ts[:, None])
    approx = W @ V
    err = np.abs(approx - exact).sum(axis=1)
    print(f"tol={tol:.0e}: N={N}, max l1 error over [0,{T:.0f}] = {err.max():.2e}, "
          f"at t={ts[err.argmax()]:.1f}; bound = {tail_bound(Lam*T, N):.2e}")

# (c) a fixed N is not uniform on [0, infinity): the error grows with t
N = choose_N(1e-6, Lam * T)
for t in (60, 120, 240):
    print(f"N={N}, t={t}: P(Poisson(Lam t) > N) = {poisson.sf(N, Lam * t):.3e}")
```

### T1
Let the stand have finitely many states and generator $Q$ ($q_{ij}\ge0$ for $i\ne j$, rows summing to $0$). Pick $\Lambda\ge\max_i(-q_{ii})$ and set $P=I+Q/\Lambda$. Take as given (Module 0.3) that $p(t)=p(0)e^{Qt}=\sum_{n\ge0}w_n(t)\,p(0)P^n$ with $w_n(t)=e^{-\Lambda t}(\Lambda t)^n/n!$, and let $p_N(t)$ be the sum up to $n=N$. Prove:
(a) $P$ is a stochastic matrix.
(b) $\|p(t)-p_N(t)\|_1=\sum_{n>N}w_n(t)=P\big(\text{Poisson}(\Lambda t)>N\big)$.
(c) This error increases in $t$, so $\sup_{0\le t\le T}\|p(t)-p_N(t)\|_1=P\big(\text{Poisson}(\Lambda T)>N\big)$: one $N$ serves the whole horizon.
(d) For the stand in D1 ($\Lambda=2.2$ per minute, $T=60$ minutes), use Lemma 4.1 to find $N$ for tolerance $10^{-6}$.

#### Hint
(b) Every vector $p(0)P^n$ is a probability vector, and all terms are nonnegative. (c) Differentiate $F(s)=\sum_{n=0}^Ne^{-s}s^n/n!$ in $s$; the sum telescopes.

#### Solution
(a) Off the diagonal, $P_{ij}=q_{ij}/\Lambda\ge0$. On the diagonal, $P_{ii}=1+q_{ii}/\Lambda\ge0$ because $\Lambda\ge-q_{ii}$. Row sums: $1+\frac1\Lambda\sum_jq_{ij}=1$. So $P$ is stochastic.

(b) If $v$ is a probability (row) vector, $vP$ has nonnegative entries summing to $\sum_iv_i\sum_jP_{ij}=1$. By induction each $v_n=p(0)P^n$ is a probability vector. Then $p(t)-p_N(t)=\sum_{n>N}w_n(t)v_n$, a vector with nonnegative entries. Its $\ell_1$ norm is the sum of its entries, and swapping the order of two sums of nonnegative terms (Section 8, double sums) gives
$$\|p(t)-p_N(t)\|_1=\sum_j\sum_{n>N}w_n(t)(v_n)_j=\sum_{n>N}w_n(t)\sum_j(v_n)_j=\sum_{n>N}w_n(t).$$
Since the $w_n(t)$ are the Poisson($\Lambda t$) probabilities, this is $P(\text{Poisson}(\Lambda t)>N)$. The error equals the bound exactly, which is why D1 matched it to three digits.

(c) Let $F(s)=\sum_{n=0}^Ne^{-s}s^n/n!$, so the error is $1-F(\Lambda t)$. Then
$$F'(s)=\sum_{n=0}^N\Big(-e^{-s}\frac{s^n}{n!}\Big)+\sum_{n=1}^Ne^{-s}\frac{s^{n-1}}{(n-1)!}=-e^{-s}\frac{s^N}{N!}<0\quad(s>0),$$
because the second sum repeats the first except for its last term. So $F$ decreases, the error $1-F(\Lambda t)$ increases in $t$, and its sup over $[0,T]$ is the value at $T$.

(d) $\Lambda T=132$. Lemma 4.1 with $N=189$: $t_{190}=3.947\times10^{-7}$, $r=132/191$, bound $1.278\times10^{-6}$: not enough. With $N=190$: $t_{191}=2.728\times10^{-7}$, $r=132/192=0.6875$, $1/(1-r)=3.2$, bound $8.73\times10^{-7}\le10^{-6}$. So $N=190$, and by (c) it works for every $t\in[0,60]$. The exact error at $t=60$ is $8.53\times10^{-7}$ (Problem D1). (Compute $t_{N+1}$ through logarithms: `math.exp(-lam + (N+1)*math.log(lam) - math.lgamma(N+2))`.)

This is a truncation rule you can state and prove in a paper, rather than a fixed number of terms. Module 3.3 extends it to time-varying rates, where the stand changes with flight banks.
